# 8. The hidden menu, again

We can now return to the floor. A 360-degree film transfers some control
over selection while retaining the recorded sphere. A conversational
character transfers some control over utterance while retaining
constraints the participant may never see. The technological mistake is
to describe either change as a scalar increase in freedom. The more
interesting transformation is that selection becomes participant-facing
while consequence remains system-governed. The frame moves. The menu
goes underground. The participant has to learn the hidden stage by
touching it.

This is why the floor and the bee belong together. The floor asks what
looking can alter. The bee asks what speaking can alter. Lucy asks
whether I can be recognized without taking over the plot. Cass asks
whether a world can understand my proposition and still refuse it. The
unreadable page asks whether a generative system can protect what it
does not know. The mystery behind Cass asks whether I can tell if I
discovered the world or helped produce the conditions of its disclosure.

These are all versions of one problem. Not how interactive is this? Not
how many things can I type? Not how intelligent did the response sound?
The smaller question cuts deeper: after I act, where is the dent? A
button prints much of its consequence in advance. The blank box does
not. That is its seduction and its danger. It offers nearly unlimited
address while concealing the grammar of alteration beneath the surface.
A theory of conversational narrative should begin there.
