# 2. The hidden menu

A conventional frame says, in effect, look here. A dialogue menu says,
say one of these. A 360-degree image removes the first visible boundary.
A blank text box removes the second. The strongest version of the
analogy with which this project began is therefore not that a chatbot is
to a book what 360 film is to film. The machinery differs too much. The
cleaner analogy concerns delegated selection: framed film is to 360 film
as menu dialogue is to free-text chat. In one case the creator gives up
complete control over view selection. In the other, the creator gives up
complete control over utterance selection. What disappears is a visible
boundary. What remains is the system underneath it (Murray 2012; Rothe,
Buschek, and Hußmann 2019).

A button advertises its verb. DELETE tells us what kind of consequence
pressing it should produce. A dialogue option labeled “Ask about the
letter” gives the participant some idea of both action and domain. The
blank box says only: speak. Speak and persuade? Speak and reveal? Speak
and change a relationship? Speak and write history? Speak and be
remembered? Speak and receive a fluent sentence whose effects end with
the sentence? Norman’s distinction between affordances and signifiers is
useful here because technical possibility and communicated possibility
are not the same thing. A text field can accept almost any string, while
the conventions of conversation signify that those strings will be
treated as meaningful contributions (Norman 2013).

The menu has not vanished. It has moved behind the conversation. This
produces the central asymmetry of open-language systems: open language
expands address before it expands alteration. The participant gains an
extraordinary ability to put things before the work, while the interface
says almost nothing about which parts of character, memory, world,
disclosure, or plot those things can change. This is why counting
possible utterances as “degrees of freedom” is a category error. The
number of strings is almost unbounded; the number of downstream
distinctions that a system can make consequential may be far smaller.

Imagine *Clouds Over Sidra* with a blank box. A viewer could ask Sidra
what she misses, what she thinks of Jordan, whether she likes football,
whether she knows quantum physics. The easy description is that the
documentary has become interactive. The more important transformation is
social. The 360 film positions the viewer as witness to recorded
testimony. A hypothetical Sidra Chat would position the viewer as an
interlocutor to generated speech. The question changes from “What does
Sidra show me?” to “What do I say to Sidra?” and then, almost
immediately, to “What is Sidra allowed to say back?” Presence becomes
reciprocal address, and reciprocal address creates an ethical and
epistemic burden the recorded film does not carry (Nash 2018; Suzuki
2022).

Conversation research makes the burden less mystical. Sacks, Schegloff,
and Jefferson described the organization of turn-taking; Clark and
Schaefer distinguished the presenting of a contribution from the work by
which participants establish what has been accepted; Clark and Brennan
treated grounding as accumulated evidence that interlocutors can proceed
together. Traum translated such problems into computational dialogue,
and Mohapatra and colleagues recently returned grounding acts and
grounding units to the problem of reliable language-model conversation
(Sacks, Schegloff, and Jefferson 1974; Clark and Schaefer 1989; Clark
and Brennan 1991; Traum 1999; Mohapatra et al. 2024). A blank box does
not abolish structure. It transfers part of the work of discovering
structure into the encounter itself.
