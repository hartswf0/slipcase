# 1. The floor

Put on *Clouds Over Sidra* and look at the floor. This is an awkward way
to watch a documentary about Sidra, a twelve-year-old Syrian girl living
in the Za’atari refugee camp, but it is a useful experiment. The
360-degree image gives the spectator something ordinary cinema usually
withholds: control over where the frame points. Chris Milk made the
removal of the rectangular frame central to the promotional language
surrounding early humanitarian VR, while subsequent work on cinematic VR
treated attention itself as a design problem because the filmmaker could
no longer guarantee that the spectator was looking where an event
occurred (Milk 2015; Rothe, Buschek, and Hußmann 2019; MIT Open
Documentary Lab 2015).

At first the lesson seems obvious. More looking looks like more agency.
The floor ruins that conclusion. If I spend the film staring downward,
Sidra continues. Nobody asks what I am doing. Nothing in the camp
changes because I have inspected the ground. I can turn my head freely
while remaining fixed at the capture position, a peculiar mobility
without entry. The XR vocabulary helps only up to a point: rotational
and positional tracking describe movement, not whether a represented
world recognizes that movement (XR Association 2020). The early zettels
called this the condition of a disembodied head on rails. The later
forage corrected the metaphor. Orientation can be real while reciprocity
remains absent.

Yet the floor does not prove that 360 documentary has failed. It proves
that we asked the wrong question. Looking can change perception without
changing story state. It can produce context, spatial witnessing,
self-directed attention, or the uneasy knowledge that something may be
happening behind one’s head. Kate Nash’s account of mediated witness in
VR makes presence an ethical problem rather than a technical
achievement, and Jihoon Kim’s analysis of virtual documentary treats
synthetic vision as a particular arrangement of seeing rather than a
shortcut to embodied access. Moe Suzuki pushes the point further in her
critique of *Clouds Over Sidra*: technological closeness can coexist
with political distance and disembodiment (Nash 2018; Kim 2021; Suzuki
2022).

This is also why the early FOMO claim needed repair. Aitamurto and
colleagues found both fear and joy in the necessity of missing parts of
a 360-degree field. Freedom to look does not automatically create fear.
It creates attention uncertainty. FOMO appears when the spectator
believes that unseen material is important, difficult to recover, and
perhaps part of a correct viewing path (Aitamurto et al. 2021). The
floor therefore gives us a better question, one that survives beyond VR:
when a work gives me an action, what is that action for? Not every
action needs to bend the plot. The consequence has to fit the
invitation.
