## The Warm Seed

I write some words.

Something answers.

I read the answer and recognize my thought in it.

Then I do not know what the word *my* means.

This is the wound.

Not whether the machine is intelligent.

Not whether the prompt is code.

Not whether a university permits it.

I have encountered a form of writing in which my intention survives while my sentences do not.

I do not yet know what this makes me.

## The Unsaid

There is a point beyond which provenance does not settle authorship.

I can show the prompt.

I can show the generations.

I can show what I rejected.

I can show every alteration.

At the end someone may still ask:

*But did you write it?*

And I may still hesitate.

The hesitation is not missing evidence.

It is the thing itself.

# APHORISTIC FRAGMENTS

**1.**

I thought the difficult question was: *What is a prompt?*

Perhaps the difficult question is why I need the prompt to be something.

A language.
A program.
A literary form.
A method.

As though naming it would permit me to continue using it without embarrassment.

---

**2.**

A prompt is words written before other words arrive.

This seems too small a definition.

I distrust the desire to enlarge it.

---

**3.**

I type:

*Make the house remember.*

The machine does something.

Before this, the sentence might have belonged to a poem.

Now I have to wonder whether it was also an instruction.

Nothing changed in the sentence.

The world around the sentence changed.

---

**4.**

Perhaps this is why the prompt feels strange.

It has not learned a new grammar.

It has acquired consequences.

---

**5.**

There are words that describe a door.

There are words that tell someone to build a door.

There are words that cause a door to appear on a screen.

The difference is not visible in the words themselves.

---

**6.**

I keep wanting to say that description has become operation.

But this is already too smooth.

A description does not become an operation.

Someone builds a machine in which describing has effects.

The machine disappears from the sentence.

This disappearance interests me more than the slogan.

---

**7.**

*Here.*

*That one.*

*Again.*

*Like before.*

A great deal of prompting consists of words that would be almost meaningless if nothing had already happened.

The prompt leans on a world.

---

**8.**

Deixis may be closer to prompting than command.

The finger is older than the instruction manual.

---

**9.**

The word *this* contains almost nothing.

Yet sometimes it is enough.

Perhaps language becomes powerful not when it contains the world, but when there is already a world to point into.

---

**10.**

Ekphrasis once meant making the absent vivid in words.

Now I describe a room and a room appears.

I am tempted to announce a revolution.

But perhaps the more frightening fact is that I become accustomed to it within an afternoon.

---

**11.**

The miraculous quickly becomes an interface.

---

**12.**

I tell myself I am studying prompting.

Sometimes I suspect I am studying what happens to responsibility when language begins doing more than I expected language to do.

---

**13.**

"Did you use AI to write this?"

The question produces irritation because it demands a yes or no where my experience contains neither.

This does not make the question stupid.

It makes my categories poor.

---

**14.**

If I answer *yes*, something important disappears.

If I answer *no*, something important disappears.

A philosophical problem sometimes begins when both ordinary answers feel dishonest.

---

**15.**

I wrote the prompt.

The model wrote the sentence.

I chose the sentence.

I changed the sentence.

I placed it here.

Which of these is writing?

I notice how badly I want one of them to be the sovereign act.

---

**16.**

We like authorship to have a throne.

Real work looks more like an ant heap.

---

**17.**

One carries a fragment.

One cuts.

One remembers a source.

One objects.

One rearranges.

One hears that the rhythm is wrong.

Then the name of a single person is printed on the cover.

We were comfortable with this because most of the ants lived inside one skull.

---

**18.**

The machine moves some of the ants outside.

Now we become metaphysicians.

---

**19.**

Perhaps the scandal is not that authorship has become distributed.

Perhaps the scandal is that its distribution has become visible.

---

**20.**

I can keep prompt receipts.

Every instruction.

Every failure.

Every branch.

Every discarded sentence.

This feels honest.

It also feels like the dream of a bureaucrat: if every movement is recorded, responsibility will finally become clear.

---

**21.**

A receipt proves that a transaction occurred.

It does not prove who desired the object.

---

**22.**

There may be no document detailed enough to answer *Did you write it?*

At some point the archive ends and a person must speak.

---

**23.**

I am attracted to the phrase *prompt receipts* because I am afraid of being accused of stealing from my own machine.

---

**24.**

The machine cannot absolve me.

This is easy to forget.

If the sentence is foolish, I cannot send the model to defend it.

---

**25.**

Responsibility may survive where production does not.

Perhaps this is one thread I can hold without pretending it is the whole rope.

---

**26.**

A director says: again, slower, from the doorway.

No one asks whether the director personally emitted the photons.

Yet I should be careful with analogies.

Every analogy is a bridge.

Soon one forgets there is a river underneath.

---

**27.**

"Call the shots."

I like the phrase because it sounds like authority.

But directing is also waiting for something you did not imagine.

If nothing can surprise the director, perhaps there is no directing.

Only execution.

---

**28.**

The prompt is sometimes a command.

Sometimes a lure.

Sometimes a fence.

Sometimes an example placed beside an opening.

Sometimes it is only a hand held out in a dark room.

---

**29.**

I wanted to distinguish *class* from *path*.

A path says: go here, then here.

A class says: anything like this will do.

But human writing already contains both.

A sonnet is a class.

This sentence is a path.

The distinction does not belong to machines.

The machine merely makes it harder to ignore.

---

**30.**

Perhaps a prompt does not write the object.

It writes the neighborhood in which the object may be found.

---

**31.**

This is attractive.

Too attractive.

One must distrust metaphors that arrive already polished.

---

**32.**

A gardener does not specify the path of each root.

He does not merely "delegate" root growth either.

The verb fails.

We often discover that our theory is weak because none of our verbs survive contact with the thing.

---

**33.**

I say "generate."

Before that I said "write."

Before that I said "make."

Each verb smuggles in a different picture of agency.

Much philosophy may be the inspection of smuggled pictures.

---

**34.**

What is the native language of the prompt?

I asked this as though somewhere there must be a grammar book.

Perhaps the prompt has no native language.

Perhaps it is precisely language spoken to something that has no native tongue.

---

**35.**

English enters.

Numbers enter.

Images enter.

Examples enter.

A gesture made by selecting part of a picture enters.

Silence sometimes enters as context.

Where exactly is the language?

---

**36.**

A programming language tells the machine what the symbols are permitted to mean.

A prompt often discovers what its symbols meant only after the machine answers.

This seems important.

I should resist improving it.

---

**37.**

In code, an error can mean the expression is invalid.

In prompting, an error can mean I have learned something about what I was trying to ask.

---

**38.**

The failed prompt is sometimes more revealing than the successful one.

The successful prompt disappears into its result.

The failure leaves the machinery exposed.

---

**39.**

A broken tool tells you its shape.

---

**40.**

I wanted a theory of viable prompts.

But perhaps viability is simply this:

the words survive contact with the machine without losing the thing I cared about.

Already I hear the objection:

What thing?

I cannot always say.

---

**41.**

The inability to state the criterion does not mean there is no criterion.

A musician stops the rehearsal and says, "No."

Everyone in the room may understand.

No metric appears.

---

**42.**

One danger of prompt research is the desire to make taste explicit.

Taste dies easily under bright laboratory light.

---

**43.**

The better prompt often comes from knowing what to leave unwritten.

This is awkward for a dissertation.

A dissertation is a machine for demanding that the unwritten explain itself.

---

**44.**

Thick description interests me because it appears to add words.

Its real achievement may be the opposite.

It makes visible how much meaning was already outside the sentence.

---

**45.**

A wink cannot be distinguished from a twitch by geometry alone.

A prompt cannot always be distinguished from an ordinary sentence by syntax alone.

Context does the work.

Context is the great unpaid laborer of language.

---

**46.**

I describe the world thickly so the machine will understand.

But perhaps what I really do is construct enough surroundings that misunderstanding becomes difficult.

---

**47.**

This resembles writing for people more than I would like.

---

**48.**

The phrase *distant writing* pleased me immediately.

This is reason enough to be suspicious of it.

---

**49.**

I do not write every sentence.

I arrange conditions under which sentences arrive.

Is this distant?

A composer does not vibrate the violin string.

Still, the music is not "distant composition."

Perhaps I am naming the strangeness rather than understanding it.

---

**50.**

A Markov poet chooses a corpus, a rule, an output.

Where is the poem?

In the corpus?

In the rule?

In the generated text?

In the selection?

The question becomes ridiculous only when one insists that the poem must be hiding in one place.

---

**51.**

We ask *who wrote this sentence?*

We rarely ask *where did this sentence become necessary?*

The second question may lead somewhere less comfortable.

---

**52.**

I fear that the graduate school will say:

This is not writing.

Beneath this fear is another:

What if they are right?

---

**53.**

A defensive theory is usually easy to recognize.

Every concept turns toward the tribunal.

---

**54.**

If I invent "prompt language" only so that prompting can sit respectably beside programming languages, the concept is already corrupted.

A chair built only to resemble the other chairs may not support weight.

---

**55.**

The question should not be:

Can prompts be admitted into the university?

The question is whether anything strange enough is happening here that the university's old words begin to wobble.

---

**56.**

If they do not wobble, perhaps there is no dissertation.

Only a petition.

---

**57.**

The strongest demonstration might be the one that makes my own position less secure.

---

**58.**

Could a prompt generate the dissertation?

At first this sounds like a stunt.

Then it becomes frightening.

Suppose it could.

What exactly would have been compressed into the prompt?

The argument?

The style?

A shadow of the argument?

A machine for producing things that resemble the argument?

---

**59.**

If I can regenerate the dissertation from a smaller symbolic object, I will be tempted to call that object the real dissertation.

This is the old worship of compression.

A seed is smaller than a tree.

It is not therefore more truly the tree.

---

**60.**

The tree contains weather.

The seed did not write the weather.

---

**61.**

A prompt that produces ten thousand words has not necessarily contained ten thousand words of thought.

Sometimes it has only opened a sluice gate.

Volume is a poor witness.

---

**62.**

I should be wary whenever computational abundance makes me feel intellectually rich.

---

**63.**

"Can a novel be a program?"

Perhaps.

But I notice another question hiding underneath:

Why do I need the novel to become a program?

Is being a novel no longer enough?

---

**64.**

The hunger to make everything executable may be another form of impatience with things that refuse us.

---

**65.**

A novel does not fail because I cannot run it.

A poem does not malfunction because two readers disagree.

Not every opacity is a defect waiting for computation.

---

**66.**

And yet I cannot forget the moment when a description begins producing consequences.

Something has happened there.

I do not want enthusiasm to exaggerate it.

I do not want caution to erase it.

---

**67.**

Between code and poetry.

Another beautiful phrase.

The danger is imagining two countries with a clean border between them.

Real language is mud.

---

**68.**

Perhaps prompting is interesting because mud has entered the machine room.

---

**69.**

Programming once seemed to promise that commands could be made exact.

Prompting introduces a machine that responds usefully to our inexactness.

This feels less like the triumph of computation than a confession.

---

**70.**

We built machines that could only obey precision.

Then we built machines that could survive us.

---

**71.**

"Are we meant to interpret?"

The strange thing is that interpretation was never optional.

We merely built some systems in which most interpretations were forbidden.

---

**72.**

A poem multiplies readings.

A program kills them.

A prompt negotiates.

This is too neat.

Strike the sentence.

---

**73.**

Sometimes a prompt multiplies readings until nothing useful remains.

Sometimes a poem is brutally exact.

Sometimes code opens an unforeseen world.

The ant heap returns whenever the theory becomes symmetrical.

---

**74.**

I want my dissertation to cohere.

This is reasonable.

I also know that coherence can become a form of lying.

A field may possess several centers because the problem itself has several centers.

---

**75.**

The desire for one master concept is often the desire to stop being confused.

But confusion may be an accurate response.

---

**76.**

"Prompt-forward dissertation."

The phrase makes me uneasy.

Good.

The uneasiness should not be removed by definition.

It should be examined until I can see what I am afraid of losing.

---

**77.**

Perhaps I fear losing the small bodily evidence of authorship:

my hands on the keyboard,

the sentence crossed out,

the night spent trying to find one verb.

---

**78.**

Old-fashioned writing leaves fatigue in the artifact's memory.

Prompting sometimes hides the fatigue.

Perhaps this is why it looks fraudulent even when enormous judgment has been exercised.

---

**79.**

We trust sweat because sweat is visible evidence of resistance.

A machine that removes resistance also removes one of our oldest proofs that work occurred.

---

**80.**

This may explain more of the moral panic than arguments about intelligence do.

---

**81.**

If writing becomes easy, we suspect that thought has become easy.

The suspicion is sometimes correct.

---

**82.**

The machine can save me from the labor that was merely labor.

It can also save me from the labor in which the thought would have appeared.

I cannot know in advance which kind I am avoiding.

---

**83.**

This is the danger I recognize in myself.

Not plagiarism.

Not automation.

Evasion.

---

**84.**

A sentence generated too quickly can allow me to pass over a place where I should have remained troubled.

---

**85.**

The old way of writing forced me to stay with certain difficulties because I had no way around them.

Now there is always a way around.

This changes the ethics of attention.

---

**86.**

Perhaps the most important prompt is sometimes:

*No. Again.*

Perhaps the most important act is sometimes refusing to prompt at all.

---

**87.**

I wanted to prove that prompts are a valid form of writing.

Now I think the word *valid* belongs to the committee, not to the phenomenon.

---

**88.**

The phenomenon does not care whether it receives credit.

---

**89.**

If the dissertation succeeds only when everyone agrees that I wrote it, then I have made the wrong thing depend on the verdict.

---

**90.**

I should be able to place the prompts on the table without pride and without apology.

Here is what I said.

Here is what came back.

Here is what I kept.

Here is what I could not make the machine understand.

The last category may matter most.

---

**91.**

The boundary of a prompt language may be visible in the things that require a life rather than a description.

---

**92.**

What do I know that the model does not know?

This question quickly becomes childish.

What does my body know?

What does my shame know?

What does thirty years of looking at faces know without being able to state it?

I cannot pour all of this into the context window.

---

**93.**

Perhaps the decisive human contribution is not a secret substance called creativity.

Perhaps it is that I am somewhere.

---

**94.**

The model can say *here*.

But I am here.

The difference sounds mystical only because our technical vocabulary becomes embarrassed near the body.

---

**95.**

A prompt begins from a position.

Who is speaking?

From where?

After what?

Toward what?

The symbols alone conceal these questions.

---

**96.**

Maybe that is why deixis keeps returning.

Not because it provides a theory of prompts.

Because *here* cannot be understood from nowhere.

---

**97.**

I can make the machine write in my voice.

This is impressive.

Then I read it and sometimes feel nothing.

The likeness of a voice is not the possession of a life.

---

**98.**

I can also write a sentence myself and feel nothing.

This prevents the comforting conclusion.

---

**99.**

There is no simple border with life on one side and machinery on the other.

The border runs through my own writing.

---

**100.**

The dissertation may not need to tell me what a prompt is.

It may need to leave me unable to use the word *write* as carelessly as I used it before.

---

**101.**

I began by asking whether prompting could count as authorship.

I end up asking why I wanted authorship to remain indivisible.

---

**102.**

Perhaps we protect the author because we need a place to put praise and blame.

The world gives us processes.

We give the process a name.

---

**103.**

Then the name becomes jealous.

---

**104.**

I can say:

*I wrote it.*

I can say:

*The model wrote it.*

Both sentences may function perfectly well in ordinary conversation.

The philosophical trouble begins only when I demand that one of them describe the whole event.

---

**105.**

No sentence describes the whole event.

That was true before the machine.

---

**106.**

What the machine has done is remove one of our excuses for forgetting it.
