TITLE:
AI may not distribute authorship so much as externalize divisions already hidden inside writing.

SOURCE:
Unattributed working manuscript — “Aphoristic Fragments,” fragments 15–19 — date not stated. fileciteturn0file0L210-L268

PASSAGE:
[QUOTE]
“We like authorship to have a throne.
Real work looks more like an ant heap.”

[QUOTE]
“Perhaps the scandal is not that authorship has become distributed.
Perhaps the scandal is that its distribution has become visible.”

RESEARCH OBJECT:
Visibility of distributed production.

LOCAL MOVE:
The manuscript rejects a before/after genealogy in which solitary authorship existed before AI and distributed authorship began afterward.

SOURCE TERMS:
authorship
throne

WHAT BECAME STRANGE:
The conceptual disruption may come from relocating some writing operations outside the bodily person, not from inventing distributed production itself.

QUESTION:
Which writing operations become newly visible as separable operations when a model performs them externally?

DEEPER QUESTION:
Was the “single author” ever an adequate process description, or primarily an attribution mechanism attached after production?

MECHANISM:
Writing already contains heterogeneous operations. When these operations occur within one person they can be compressed under a single author-name. Externalizing some operations makes the decomposition difficult to ignore.

FORMAL SHIFT:
<apparently unitary author>
→ <decomposed writing operations>
→ [externalize selected operations]
→ <distribution becomes inspectable>

SOURCE FORMALISM:
NONE

OUR FORMALIZATION:
[OUR FORMALIZATION — NOT SOURCE SYNTAX]
WRITING EVENT =
generate + reject + remember + revise + arrange + evaluate + contextualize + ...

The author-name does not specify the allocation of these operations.

TENSION:
Externalizing an operation may change the operation itself. The fact that writing was already internally heterogeneous does not prove that human-model collaboration is merely more of the same.

MISSING:
A comparison between operations performed “inside one skull” and apparently analogous operations performed by other people, institutions, software, or generative models.

BOUNDARY:
The passage does not show that all participants in distributed production are authors, nor that authorship should be abandoned.

CITATION TRAIL:
Distributed cognition; sociology of authorship; textual scholarship; collaborative writing; film production; actor-network approaches; genetic criticism.

TEST:
Take a human-only manuscript revision history and a human-model revision history. Decompose each into observable operations without initially labeling any actor “author.” Compare what genuinely new operations or allocations appear.
