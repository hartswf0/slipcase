# BETWEEN CODE AND POETRY

## Committee notes toward a prompt-forward dissertation

The dissertation needs to cohere around a single problem: **what kind of writing is a prompt?**

That question connects the deictic, the ekphrastic, programming languages, literary writing, interactive narrative, thick description, AI authorship, and the practical problem of how the dissertation itself should be written and evaluated.

The wager is that the prompt is not merely an instruction attached to a more important artifact. It is potentially **a form of writing in its own right**.

A prompt is a set of symbols that enters into a model and changes what can happen next. But that minimal definition does not tell us what kind of symbolic object it is. A prompt can describe, point, specify, constrain, invoke, establish a context, declare a class of acceptable outcomes, prescribe a path, or construct an entire space of possible continuations.

It therefore sits strangely **between code and poetry**.

Code conventionally specifies operations with formal semantics. Poetry works through language without requiring deterministic execution. A prompt occupies the unsettled territory between them: language written to be interpreted, but interpreted by a computational model in order to produce further language, images, actions, programs, or worlds.

The dissertation can make that unstable middle ground its object.

---

# 1. The central question

**WHAT IS A PROMPT?**

At the weakest level:

> A prompt is a set of symbols that enters into a model.

But that definition is deliberately insufficient. Plenty of symbol sequences enter computational systems without becoming prompts.

The stronger question is:

**What is distinctive about a symbolic expression written for interpretation by a generative model?**

What are its language requirements?

What makes a prompt viable?

What constitutes success or failure?

What is its relationship to ordinary language?

What is its relationship to code?

What is its relationship to literary form?

Does a prompt specify a **class** of possible outputs or a **path** through a generative system?

And perhaps most importantly:

**What is the native language of the prompt?**

Not English. Not Python. Not necessarily any named programming language.

The native language of the prompt may be a hybrid language whose semantics emerge from interaction among natural language, model architecture, training distributions, context, examples, system constraints, tool affordances, and iterative feedback.

To speak the language of the prompt is therefore not simply to "talk to AI."

It is to learn how descriptions become operative.

---

# 2. Deixis and ekphrasis

Two older linguistic and literary concepts provide an important bridge.

## DEIXIS

Deictic language points.

*Here.*
*There.*
*This.*
*That.*
*Now.*
*You.*
*The object on the left.*
*Continue from the previous scene.*

Its meaning depends upon a situation.

A deictic expression cannot be fully interpreted without knowing **where the speaker stands, what has already happened, who is being addressed, and what objects are currently available for reference**.

Prompting is deeply deictic.

A prompt is often less like an autonomous text than an intervention into an already-existing computational situation:

> Make **this** darker.
> Continue **that** argument.
> Preserve **the previous style**.
> Move **it** closer.
> Do the same thing **again**, but from above.

The prompt therefore does not merely contain meaning. It establishes a relation between language and an active context.

## EKPHRASIS

Ekphrasis traditionally concerns the verbal description of something visual or otherwise absent: making something present through words.

Generative systems radicalize this relation.

Description no longer merely evokes an imagined object in the mind of a reader.

Description can become an input into machinery that **constructs an artifact**.

The ekphrastic relation therefore becomes operational.

The description begins to behave like an instrument.

This is the larger dissertation territory:

> When the world must pass through representations in order for us to act in it, description ceases to be secondary to reality and becomes part of reality's operating machinery.

Prompting is one especially visible instance of that transition.

# 3. Call the shots

**CALL THE SHOTS** could become one of the dissertation's major conceptual chapters or case studies.

The phrase matters because it captures the strange authorship structure of prompting.

The director does not personally:

- move the camera,
- build the set,
- perform the scene,
- focus the lens,
- edit every frame,
- manufacture every prop.

Yet the director is not therefore absent from the resulting work.

The director **calls the shots**.

Prompting raises a comparable but unfamiliar division of intellectual labor.

Someone writes:

> Low camera.
> Thirty-five-millimeter lens.
> Actor remains still.
> Let the crowd move behind her.
> Hold two seconds longer than feels comfortable.

The person has not produced the pixels directly.

But neither has the person merely pressed a button.

The prompt becomes a consequential authored structure determining what is attempted, rejected, repeated, preserved, and transformed.

This produces an authorship problem because our cultural intuitions about writing remain tied to direct inscription:

> There is nothing but words that **I write**.

If I manually type every sentence of a chapter, conventional academic authorship is obvious.

If I instead create a system of descriptions, constraints, examples, revisions, selections, and transformations from which the final prose emerges, observers may feel that I did not write it.

But the dissertation can ask whether this distinction remains intellectually coherent.

---

# 4. The existential problem

Jay's concern is not superficial.

There is a genuine institutional problem:

**Will a graduate school recognize a dissertation whose principal intellectual contribution is expressed through prompts as legitimate scholarship?**

The question resembles the old problem of pinball:

> Is pinball a game of skill or a game of chance?

The interesting answer is not obtained by choosing one side abstractly.

You open the machine.

You identify which outcomes are controlled, which are stochastic, what operations are available to the player, how skilled action changes the probability distribution, and where agency actually resides.

The same move is available here.

Instead of asking:

> Did AI write this?

ask:

> **What exactly did the researcher contribute?**

The dissertation should make that contribution inspectable.

---

# 5. Prompt receipts

This suggests a methodological and rhetorical device:

# PROMPT RECEIPTS

Do not conceal the prompts.

**Submit them.**

Treat prompts as research materials.

For major case studies, preserve:

- initial prompts,
- failed prompts,
- branching prompts,
- revisions,
- constraints,
- generated candidates,
- selection decisions,
- discarded outputs,
- transformations,
- final outputs,
- commentary explaining why one intervention followed another.

The appendix could contain extensive prompt receipts.

A chapter might show only the decisive exchanges, while the full interaction history remains available as evidence.

That changes the argument from:

> Trust me, I wrote this.

to:

> Here is the operative record. Determine where the intellectual work occurred.

This could become a powerful answer to the authorship objection because the dissertation does not attempt to make computational assistance invisible.

It makes the **distribution of agency legible**.

---

# 6. "Did you use AI to write this chapter?"

The dissertation should probably confront this question explicitly rather than attempting to evade it.

**Did you use AI to write this chapter?**

Yes—but that answer is too coarse to tell us anything useful.

Compare:

**Did you use Microsoft Word to write this chapter?**

**Did you use a search engine?**

**Did you use citation software?**

**Did you dictate part of it?**

**Did another scholar edit it?**

**Did a programming language generate a graph that appears in it?**

Different technologies participate in writing differently.

The important scholarly question is not whether a machine entered the causal chain.

It is:

> **What operations were delegated, what operations were retained, and where did the contribution reside?**

The prompt receipts become evidence.

---

# 7. "I wrote it"

There is a difficult phenomenological question underneath the institutional one.

Suppose two scholars use identical systems.

One says:

> In my mind, I wrote it.

The other says:

> In my mind, I didn't write it.

What explains the difference?

Direct keystrokes clearly cannot be the entire criterion.

People already say:

> I wrote a book

when an editor changed sentences.

They say:

> I made a film

without personally exposing every frame.

They say:

> I wrote the program

although compilers, libraries, operating systems, and processors produced most of the machine operations.

So what exactly produces the feeling:

**I wrote it**?

Possibilities include:

- origination of intention,
- control of structure,
- selection,
- specification,
- revision,
- responsibility,
- recognizable voice,
- causal influence,
- knowledge of why the artifact has its present form.

This question may become more philosophically interesting than the simplistic authorship debate.

---

# 8. Ontologically unsound categories

The danger is that existing institutional categories may be **ontologically unsound** for describing this activity.

"Written by AI."

"Written by the student."

These appear to describe mutually exclusive origins.

But generative work may instead consist of layered operations:

**human → prompt → model → output → human judgment → prompt → model → selection → revision → composition**

There may be no single point at which the work changes ontological ownership.

The problem is similar to asking whether a building was made by the architect or the crane.

The categories are operating at different levels of description.

A major contribution of the dissertation may therefore be to produce a better vocabulary for **distributed textual production**.

---

# 9. Prompt-forward scholarship

The dissertation does not need to hide its prompts behind prose written "the old-fashioned way."

In fact, doing so may weaken the research.

There is an important distinction between:

**A dissertation ABOUT prompting written conventionally**

and

**a PROMPT-FORWARD dissertation whose own form demonstrates the argument.**

The second is considerably more provocative.

The danger is obvious: the experiment can become so radical that reviewers stop evaluating the intellectual argument and instead debate whether the artifact qualifies as a dissertation.

The design problem is therefore not maximal novelty.

It is **legible novelty**.

The conventional dissertation can serve as an infrastructure capable of supporting unconventional forms of evidence.

That suggests a layered architecture:

**Main chapters:** rigorous argumentative prose.

**Case studies:** prompts operating as scholarly objects.

**Prompt receipts:** demonstrations of contribution.

**Appendices:** complete interactions where useful.

**Executable artifacts:** applications, interactive stories, generators, prompt languages.

In other words:

> Do not ask the committee to believe prompts are scholarship before they have seen what prompts can do.

Show them.

---

# 10. Distant writing

"Distant writing" may become a crucial term.

Franco Moretti's distant reading described literary analysis conducted at scales where individual texts cease to be the primary unit of attention.

**Distant writing** could name a complementary inversion:

> writing in which the writer increasingly operates upon structures capable of producing text rather than inscribing every sentence directly.

The writer moves upward in abstraction.

Instead of choosing every word:

> establish this argumentative structure.

Instead of composing every paragraph:

> produce three rival formulations preserving these propositions.

Instead of manually revising ten thousand words:

> detect where the argument changes definitions and reconcile the terminology without removing the distinction.

The author becomes partly a designer of generative conditions.

Yet distant writing need not mean **less authorship**.

It may mean authorship exercised at another scale.

This provides a bridge between prompting and programming.

---

# 11. Markov poet

**MARKOV POET** could provide an important historical counterexample.

Long before contemporary language models, writers and artists built stochastic text generators from Markov chains.

These systems made the authorship problem visible in primitive form.

If I:

1. choose the corpus,
2. design the transition mechanism,
3. select the order of the chain,
4. run ten thousand generations,
5. choose fourteen lines,
6. arrange them into a poem,

who wrote the poem?

There is no satisfying answer if "writing" means only "producing the immediate sequence of words."

The Markov poet therefore creates a historical path from procedural literature to contemporary prompting.

It also prevents the dissertation from looking like a theory invented solely to rationalize current AI practice.

---

# 12. From ALGOL to prompt language

ALGOL and other formal programming languages offer another historical anchor.

Programming languages established that writing can simultaneously be:

- symbolic,
- procedural,
- formal,
- expressive,
- executable,
- intended for humans,
- intended for machines.

Their emergence required new ideas about notation, semantics, interpretation, abstraction, and correctness.

Prompt languages may represent another moment in that history.

But their defining characteristics differ.

A conventional programming language tries to minimize interpretive ambiguity.

A prompt language often **works through controlled ambiguity**.

Its semantics may be probabilistic.

Its execution depends heavily on context.

Equivalent surface forms may diverge.

Different expressions may converge.

Meaning can be demonstrated operationally without being exhaustively specified formally.

The comparison is therefore not:

> prompts are code.

The more productive claim is:

> **prompts force us to reconsider what counts as a language for computation.**

---

# 13. Language requirements

A central theoretical chapter could ask what minimum properties constitute a **prompt language**.

Possible requirements:

### 1. Expressions

There must be some class of expressions that can be supplied to a generative system.

### 2. Interpretation

Different expressions must be capable of producing systematically different model behavior.

### 3. Composition

Expressions must be combinable into larger structures.

### 4. Reference

The language must allow writers to identify entities, contexts, prior outputs, desired properties, or operations.

### 5. Constraint

The writer must be capable of narrowing a space of possible outputs.

### 6. Transformation

Expressions must be capable of requesting changes to existing states.

### 7. Iteration

Outputs must be capable of becoming inputs to subsequent operations.

### 8. Pragmatics

Interpretation must depend partly upon context rather than syntax alone.

### 9. Evaluation

There must be some way to distinguish viable prompts from nonviable prompts relative to an intended effect.

This creates a serious research program rather than a collection of prompting tricks.

---

# 14. Viable prompts

The concept of the **viable prompt** may be especially useful.

A prompt need not deterministically specify its result.

It needs to reliably maintain a region of possibility within which useful results occur.

That shifts evaluation away from:

> Did this prompt generate precisely one predetermined answer?

toward:

> Does this prompt construct and maintain the intended class of outcomes?

This returns us to:

# CLASS VS. PATH

A conventional program often specifies a path.

A generative prompt may instead define a class.

For example:

> Write a detective story in which every apparent supernatural event eventually receives a mundane explanation.

This does not specify one story.

It specifies a **space of stories**.

The prompt's expressive object is therefore not necessarily the generated artifact.

The object may be the **possibility space itself**.

That is a major theoretical claim.

---

# 15. Class versus path

This distinction could provide one of the cleanest formal axes in the dissertation.

A **path specification** tells the machine what sequence of operations to perform.

A **class specification** tells the machine what kind of resulting object should count.

Traditional programming leans toward path specification:

> Do A, then B, then C.

Generative prompting often leans toward class specification:

> Produce an object satisfying conditions X, Y, and Z.

But prompts can contain both.

The history of prompt engineering might therefore be understood partly as learning when to specify a path and when to specify a class.

This connects directly to creative authorship.

A novelist traditionally determines both the class and nearly every path through the text.

A prompt-forward author can increasingly author the **class** while delegating portions of the path.

---

# 16. Where am I taking you?

Another useful distinction is between two forms of textual control.

**Where am I taking you?**

versus

**What kind of place am I constructing for you to move through?**

Traditional linear prose is highly path-oriented.

The author determines sentence one, then sentence two, then sentence three.

Interactive stories complicate this.

Games complicate it further.

Generative systems make the distinction impossible to ignore.

An author can design a field of possible paths rather than one path.

That does not eliminate authorship.

It changes its object.

---

# 17. Interactive stories

Interactive storytelling therefore matters not as an adjacent topic but as intellectual precedent.

Interactive-fiction authors have long faced questions such as:

- Is the story the authored text?
- Is the story the possibility space?
- Is the player's path the work?
- Is the underlying system the work?
- Who determines the resulting narrative?
- What counts as authorship when readers act?

Prompting extends those questions into writing generally.

The prompt writer increasingly constructs a textual system through which possible outputs can be navigated.

---

# 18. Thick description

Anthropological **thick description** is another important bridge.

Thick description demonstrates that description is not passive transcription.

To describe an action adequately is already to situate it within layers of context, interpretation, convention, intention, and consequence.

Prompting makes the stakes of description computationally visible.

Thin description:

> A man closes one eye.

Thick description:

> A conspirator deliberately winks at an accomplice to signal that the plan should proceed.

For a human interpreter, these descriptions construct different meanings.

For a generative model, they may construct different worlds.

The question becomes:

> What happens when thick description acquires execution semantics?

This may be one of the most powerful formulations available to the project.

---

# 19. The nonfiction essay as executable description

One provocative case study would test the boundary between:

**TEXT NONFICTION ESSAY**

and

**PROGRAM.**

Could an extended discourse simultaneously explain a system and specify enough of that system for a model to instantiate it?

Imagine an essay whose prose describes:

- actors,
- relations,
- constraints,
- motivations,
- environments,
- causal dynamics,
- exceptions,
- historical context.

Normally we call this description.

But if a sufficiently capable model can interpret that discourse and enact the described system, the distinction between documentation and program becomes unstable.

The question then becomes:

> **When does description become executable?**

---

# 20. A novel as application

Likewise:

**Can a novel be an application or program by itself?**

Not because the text secretly contains JavaScript.

Because an extended discourse can establish enough entities, rules, relations, temporal structures, and affordances that a generative interpreter can operate the described world.

A novel becomes not just something a machine reads.

It becomes a specification from which a world can continue.

That would be a striking demonstration of **operative ekphrasis**.

---

# 21. Are we meant to interpret?

Programming traditionally tries to delimit interpretation.

Literature depends upon it.

Prompting sits between them.

This produces the question:

# ARE WE MEANT TO INTERPRET?

When a programmer reads:

`x = x + 1`

interpretive freedom is deliberately constrained.

When a poet writes:

> the house remembers

interpretive openness is productive.

When a prompt writer writes:

> Make the house behave as though it remembers everyone who has lived there.

the phrase must simultaneously function as evocative language **and** operative specification.

The most interesting prompts may therefore behave precisely like language located between code and poetry.

---

# 22. Toxin, poison, pathology

The dissertation should resist allowing the cultural debate over AI to dictate its ontology.

Terms such as:

**toxin**
**poison**
**pathology**
**slop**

may describe real aesthetic or social phenomena.

But they can also prevent analysis by deciding in advance that generative production is contamination.

The scholarly problem is not to demonstrate that AI writing is good.

It is to determine:

> What kind of symbolic practice prompting actually is.

A technology can produce terrible novels and still create a theoretically important transformation in writing.

---

# 23. The uncomfortable experiment

One provocative experiment would be:

> **Can a prompt generate the dissertation?**

Not as a stunt in which the researcher types:

> Write my dissertation.

Instead, progressively formalize the research until the dissertation's conceptual architecture can be represented as a prompt system capable of regenerating a recognizable version of the argument.

That system might contain:

- research objects,
- propositions,
- sources,
- conceptual dependencies,
- chapter functions,
- counterarguments,
- stylistic constraints,
- evidence requirements,
- transformations,
- prohibited shortcuts.

The resulting object would reveal something astonishing.

If the prompt can regenerate the dissertation, perhaps the prompt encodes something very close to **the dissertation's intellectual structure**.

Then the question becomes:

> Which is the deeper scholarly artifact: the 80,000-word realization or the generative structure capable of producing and transforming it?

No need to answer that question completely.

Making it difficult to answer may be enough.

---

# 24. Ten thousand words

A useful practical experiment may be to work at the scale of approximately **10,000 words**.

Take one substantial argument.

Produce it traditionally.

Then produce a prompt-forward version in which the intellectual architecture, evidence, transformations, and evaluative criteria are explicitly encoded.

Compare:

- conceptual fidelity,
- argumentative coherence,
- novelty,
- revisability,
- traceability,
- authorial control,
- time,
- error,
- stylistic distinctiveness.

The purpose is not merely to show that AI is faster.

The interesting question is:

> **Can prompt-forward writing represent scholarly intention better than manually written prose?**

That claim would need evidence.

---

# 25. Prove that the form is better

This must not become an assertion that prompting is simply the future.

The dissertation needs situations in which prompt-forward composition demonstrably provides capabilities unavailable—or prohibitively difficult—in conventional writing.

For example:

**Regeneration:** alter one premise and propagate its consequences through an argument.

**Variation:** instantiate multiple rhetorically distinct versions while preserving a conceptual invariant.

**Inspection:** expose the requirements that generated a section.

**Simulation:** execute a description rather than merely discuss it.

**Scale:** operate upon ten thousand textual units without treating each independently.

**Interactivity:** permit readers to explore alternative paths through an argument.

**Refactoring:** modify the structure of discourse while preserving source commitments.

If these operations matter, prompt-forward writing has demonstrated something stronger than convenience.

---

# 26. Putative chapter architecture

A provisional dissertation could cohere across multiple disciplines without becoming a survey.

### CHAPTER 1 — WHAT IS A PROMPT?

Define the research object. Distinguish prompts from ordinary language, commands, queries, code, and instructions.

### CHAPTER 2 — BETWEEN CODE AND POETRY

Programming languages, literary language, interpretation, ambiguity, formal semantics, probabilistic semantics, ALGOL, procedural writing, interactive fiction.

### CHAPTER 3 — OPERATIVE EKPHRASIS

Description becomes operation. Ekphrasis, deixis, thick description, world specification, representation as machinery.

### CHAPTER 4 — CLASS VS. PATH

Develop a formal distinction between specifying procedures and specifying possibility spaces. Define viable prompts and generative authorship.

### CHAPTER 5 — DISTANT WRITING

Markov poets, procedural literature, generative systems, scale, abstraction, writing through structures rather than individual sentences.

### CHAPTER 6 — CALL THE SHOTS

Authorship and delegated execution. Director, architect, programmer, editor, prompt writer. Where does contribution reside?

### CHAPTER 7 — PROMPT RECEIPTS

Methodological chapter. Make generative scholarship inspectable. Develop provenance and contribution records.

### CHAPTER 8 — CASE STUDIES

Use prompt languages to construct several different objects: nonfiction argument, interactive story, world, program, perhaps a novel that operates as an application.

### CHAPTER 9 — THE PROMPT-FORWARD DISSERTATION

Turn the method back upon the dissertation. What happens when the dissertation becomes partially generative?

### CHAPTER 10 — WHAT IS THE NATIVE LANGUAGE OF THE PROMPT?

Return to the opening question with a stronger theory of computational language, interpretation, writing, and authorship.

# 27. Infrastructure

The research infrastructure should therefore preserve more than final outputs.

Its fundamental unit might be:

**PROMPT → CONTEXT → GENERATION → SELECTION → TRANSFORMATION → CLAIM**

Every meaningful output should retain a lineage.

That infrastructure allows the dissertation to answer:

**What prompt?**

**What did you know at this point?**

**What did the system produce?**

**What did you reject?**

**What did you alter?**

**Why?**

**What intellectual decision belonged to you?**

That is more informative than simply attaching a label saying:

> AI-assisted.

---

# 28. Showing contribution

The dissertation's defense of prompt-forward scholarship must ultimately rest on visible contribution.

Not:

> Believe that I authored this because I say I did.

Instead:

> Here is the architecture of decisions through which this work came into existence.

The contribution can reside in:

- formulation of the research problem,
- invention of the prompt language,
- system architecture,
- constraints,
- source selection,
- model interrogation,
- iterative direction,
- evaluative criteria,
- rejection,
- recombination,
- theoretical interpretation,
- final responsibility.

The prompts themselves may be scholarly contributions.

Some may be **methods**.

Some may be **arguments**.

Some may be **instruments**.

Some may be **programs written in an emerging language**.

---

# 29. The committee problem

The committee does not merely need reassurance that the dissertation contains enough human-written prose.

It needs a principled answer to a harder question:

> **Why should this activity count as doctoral research?**

The strongest answer is not:

> Because AI is allowed.

Nor:

> Because I actually did a lot of work.

It is:

> **Because designing, testing, theorizing, and documenting operative descriptions is the research contribution itself.**

If the dissertation studies prompting while concealing its own prompting behind prose deliberately produced in the "old-fashioned way," it may fail to test its central claim.

The form and argument should therefore meet.

But they should meet in a way that lets the committee inspect the work rather than merely trust it.

---

# 30. The dissertation's deepest question

Behind the anxieties about AI authorship is a much older question:

**WHAT IS WRITING?**

Is writing the physical inscription of words?

The originating of meanings?

The arrangement of symbols?

The production of a text?

The establishment of constraints?

The design of a possibility space?

The assumption of responsibility for an artifact?

Prompting makes these distinctions newly visible because it separates activities that conventional writing bundled together.

The dissertation does not need to prove that prompting replaces writing.

A much more interesting argument is available:

> **Prompting reveals that writing was never one operation.**

And the appearance of generative models gives us an instrument for pulling those operations apart.

That is why the prompt matters.

Not because it is a shortcut to prose.

Because it exposes the machinery of authorship.
