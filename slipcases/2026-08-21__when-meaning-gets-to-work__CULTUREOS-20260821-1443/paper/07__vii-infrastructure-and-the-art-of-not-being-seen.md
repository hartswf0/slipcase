# VII. Infrastructure and the Art of Not Being Seen

Infrastructure becomes analytically interesting at the moment it disappears into ordinary use. Star's ethnography of infrastructure emphasizes that infrastructures are embedded, learned as part of membership, linked to conventions and standards, and often become visible on breakdown. [@star1999ethnography] Star and Ruhleder sharpen the point by asking not *what* infrastructure is but *when* something becomes infrastructural in relation to organized practice. [@starruhleder1996steps]

The same water system can be background for a cook and the object of labor for a plumber. A database is invisible infrastructure to one user and an intensely visible problem to the administrator repairing it. A cultural form can move in and out of this status as well. A grammatical rule, a credential, a kin category, a calendar convention, a file format, or a bureaucratic code may recede from attention as long as it continues to support expected action. Breakdown reverses the figure and ground.

This suggests a family resemblance between infrastructure and common sense. Geertz's common sense derives authority partly from presenting historically particular judgments as the simple nature of things. Infrastructure derives much of its practical power from becoming an unremarkable condition of activity. The resemblance should not be overstated: one concerns epistemic style, the other relational material organization. But both direct attention toward a peculiar achievement of culture - the making of mediation into background.

Infrastructure also makes visible the difference between presence and accessibility. A rule may exist but not be usable by a newcomer. A database may exist but remain inaccessible without credentials. A document may be public but unreadable without the institutional competence to interpret its categories. Star and Ruhleder's work is valuable here because a technically successful tool can still fail to become infrastructure if local practices, resources, communication, and organization do not align.

The concept therefore refuses to behave as a fixed type. Something is infrastructural *for* someone, *within* a practice, *under* particular conditions, and *for* some period. This relationality matters for cultural systems more generally. The same artifact can be a symbol, a tool, a medium, an infrastructure, an obstacle, or an object of interpretation depending on where one stands in the arrangement.
