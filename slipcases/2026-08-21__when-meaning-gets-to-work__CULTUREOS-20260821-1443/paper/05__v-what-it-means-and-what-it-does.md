# V. What It Means and What It Does

Rappaport's Tsembaga ritual cycle makes one of the most important distinctions in the entire field. A ritual can have one set of meanings for participants and another set of materially describable consequences within a larger ecological and social system. [@rappaport1967ritual; @rappaport1968pigs] Pigs are slaughtered, meat distributed, alliances activated, labor burdens altered, relations to land and warfare reorganized. These consequences do not become the "real meaning" of the ritual, and participant understandings do not become errors simply because an analyst can model other effects.

This gives us two descriptions of one event that must not be collapsed: the cognized model through which people orient their action, and an operational model of relations the analyst constructs for another purpose. The separation is epistemologically uncomfortable, as it should be. Analysts have no automatic right to treat their operational model as the final truth of the situation. But the distinction remains useful because it prevents a different reduction: assuming that an adequate account of meaning has thereby accounted for consequence.

The problem appears in law whenever a litigant's understanding of a dispute diverges from the institutional form in which the dispute becomes judiciable. It appears in medicine when the lived experience of illness intersects with professional diagnostic categories and treatment infrastructures. It appears in accounting when a representation designed to render organizational activity calculable feeds back into allocation decisions. It appears in cannibalism, in Sanday's formulation, with unusual literalness: a culturally organized theory of order is enacted upon bodies, so the distance between sign and material transformation cannot be kept abstract. [@sanday1986divine]

Leslie White makes the material question impossible to bracket by treating cultural systems as energetic systems. [@white1975concept] Smil, from a very different tradition, supplies the necessary caution: energy is a universal requirement of material accomplishment without being a sufficient explanation of cultural form. [@smil2017energy] The point is almost banal, which is why it is useful. No ritual, archive, server, court, orchestra, bureaucracy, television network, or university executes without bodies, fuel, food, electricity, buildings, paper, cables, maintenance, and time. But the energy budget does not tell us whether the event will be a funeral or a football match.

The field therefore needs a distinction between enabling conditions and form-generating mechanisms. Energy, material substrate, time, and infrastructure may be necessary to execution while remaining radically underspecified with respect to what is executed. Theories that ignore them risk describing symbolic systems that float free of the conditions of their persistence. Theories that reduce everything to them lose precisely the symbolic differences anthropology was trying to preserve.

Rappaport's double description - what actors take themselves to be doing and what changes in a broader operational field - offers a way through the impasse. The two accounts can be kept distinct and then related. A cultural runtime, in this sense, is not a hidden causal machine beneath meaning. It is the ethnographic reconstruction of the points at which meaningful action enters relations whose consequences are not exhausted by the actor's own account.
