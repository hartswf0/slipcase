# XII. A Small Proposal

We may now return to Paidjan's funeral with less machinery and, I hope, more precision.

The funeral did not fail because meaning disappeared. Nor did it fail because two abstract boxes called Culture and Society collided. It failed because a recognizable symbolic sequence depended upon a practical relation that had changed. An office expected to carry a ritual obligation had been caught inside a new political-religious authorization. The cultural form and the organization of action had drifted out of register. The break made visible what ordinary performance had hidden: the rite consisted not only of symbols and sentiments but of authorized persons, timing, offices, expectations, objects, trained competences, and the local capacity to improvise when one of these no longer lined up.

This is not so much a correction of Geertz as a way of reading one line of his work through another. The early essay gives us incongruity and failure. The later essays give us public symbols, models of and models for, cultivated dispositions, common-sense obviousness, sensibility, ideology, and legal fact-making. His essay on man gives us external control mechanisms, the plans, recipes, rules, and instructions upon which human action depends. Taken together they suggest a question interpretive anthropology did not need to ask in the vocabulary of computing but which becomes difficult to avoid once symbolic systems are embedded in databases, interfaces, schedules, software, sensor systems, and other technical infrastructures: **how does a publicly available description acquire a practical career?**

I propose *cultural runtime* as a deliberately modest name for the answer one reconstructs in a particular case: **the ensemble of persons, offices, artifacts, classifications, temporal arrangements, material supports, learned capacities, interfaces, and repair practices through which a symbolic form becomes capable of making a difference to what happens next.**

The definition is intentionally cumbersome. Short definitions are often purchased by hiding the machinery. A runtime is not another cultural object beside religion, art, law, common sense, medicine, or education. It is a way of asking what any of these needs, in some concrete situation, to move from description toward consequence. Sometimes the answer will be an institution. Sometimes an artifact. Sometimes a trained perception, a schedule, a database, a ritual office, a body, a cable, a political instruction, or a locally recognized exception. Frequently it will be several of these held together without anybody possessing the whole.

The term marks four distinctions that are easy to blur. First, **representation is not consequence**: a classification can describe without yet allocating, authorizing, excluding, or moving a resource. Second, **specification is not action**: rules and plans require situated completion. Third, **coherence is not consensus**: coordinated activity can be produced among actors who know different things, hold different models, and meet only at partial interfaces. Fourth, **continuity is not stasis**: systems often persist because repair, exception, and local modification change them fast enough to keep them going.

None of this requires us to believe that culture is software. Software, if anything, is one unusually explicit modern instance of the more general problem: symbolic instructions embedded in material arrangements, distributed across specialized actors and machines, dependent upon infrastructures, incomplete without runtime conditions, and revealed with particular cruelty when something breaks. The traffic between anthropology and computing should therefore run both ways. Anthropology can prevent software metaphors from becoming fantasies of complete specification; software can lend anthropology an exacting vocabulary for asking where state is kept, who is authorized, what interfaces must hold, what dependencies have gone missing, and how an arrangement manages to continue after the world fails to match its model.

The wager is not that *runtime* should become another grand term. It probably should not. The wager is that the next time a funeral will not proceed, a category cannot contain its case, a rule loses its authorized executor, a system works only through a patch nobody documents, or a supposedly natural distinction suddenly becomes visible as infrastructure, we will learn to look not only for what the signs mean but for the arrangements that let them get to work.
