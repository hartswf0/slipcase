# I. The Grammar of "As"

The word *system* entered postwar anthropology already burdened with several incompatible ambitions. For Parsons and Shils, the cultural, social, and personality systems were analytically separable organizations within a general theory of action; Kroeber and Parsons later described culture and social system as two analytically distinct selections from the same concrete phenomena. [@parsonsshils1951action; @kroeberparsons1958concepts] Geertz's 1957 essay belongs to this analytic world before it belongs to the later world of "thick description." Drawing also upon Sorokin's distinction between logico-meaningful and causal-functional modes of integration, it asks how a single event can be organized meaningfully in one register and functionally in another, and what happens when the two organizations cease to coincide. [@sorokin1937dynamics; @geertz1957ritual]

This genealogy makes the little word *as* in Geertz's later titles newly interesting. "Religion as a Cultural System" need not be read as an ontological declaration that Religion is a bounded thing of the natural kind Cultural System. It can also be read as an instruction: take religious life under the description of its symbolic organization; ask what conceptions of order it formulates, what dispositions it cultivates, and what authority it lends those conceptions. [@geertz1966religion] "Common Sense as a Cultural System" performs a different turn: it asks how historically constructed judgments present themselves as though no construction had occurred, with the air of obviousness Geertz associated with the naturalness of common sense. [@geertz1975commonsense] "Art as a Cultural System" makes the object inseparable from the culturally trained capacity to perceive it. [@geertz1976art] In "Local Knowledge," law becomes a mode of rendering occurrences into facts that can be argued, classified, and judged. [@geertz1983law]

The temptation is to convert these essays into a neat list of operations: religion sacralizes; ideology politicizes; common sense naturalizes; art sensitizes; law adjudicates. Such a list is useful only until it begins to sound like explanation. The important fact is that each formation arranges the traffic between description and action differently. Religion joins a picture of order to a way of inhabiting that order. Ideology gives political circumstances a symbolic formulation that interest or strain theories cannot by themselves supply. Common sense removes the marks of mediation from its conclusions. Art helps constitute the sensor through which its differences can be registered. Law makes events available to institutional decision by putting them into forms of fact.

This is why the field of "X as a cultural system" cannot be treated as a simple genealogy of titles. The same phrase appears within rival traditions. Leslie White's cultural systems are material, energetic, and evolutionary in a way Geertz's symbolic ones are not. [@white1975concept] Keesing's 1974 survey already had to distinguish adaptive, cognitive, structural, and symbolic systems because the noun *system* was shared by theories that disagreed about nearly everything else. [@keesing1974theories] Later exact-title descendants - mathematics, sport, television, theater, education, the university - do not therefore belong to one family simply because their titles rhyme. They must be asked what kind of relation makes their elements systematic and what role the word *cultural* is doing in the analysis.

The same caution applies within Geertz. The five-part definition of religion is not automatically an invariant engine for ideology, common sense, and art. What travels more plausibly is a looser concern with public symbolic forms that make situations available under particular descriptions and thereby orient conduct. The "system" may lie less in a fixed collection of components than in the organized relation among models, dispositions, classifications, perceptions, institutions, and actions. The funeral at Modjokuto is useful precisely because the relation breaks while many of the components remain.

The move from "is" to "as" also has a consequence for worldmaking. Goodman argued that worlds are made from worlds already on hand: composition, decomposition, weighting, ordering, deletion, supplementation, and relabeling begin from prior versions rather than from a representation-free reality. [@goodman1978ways] A cultural description is therefore not guaranteed to remain a passive view. Once a classification is put into a form, a legal category, a database column, a curriculum, a policy, or an interface, the description can return to the world as a condition of further action. The analyst's innocent projection and the institution's operative classification are not the same thing, but they belong to the same family of problems: descriptions can acquire careers.
