---
title: "When Meaning Gets to Work"
subtitle: "Cultural Systems, Practical Arrangements, and the Life of Symbols"
author: "Watson Hartsoe"
date: "Working Paper - August 21, 2026"
abstract: |
  The phrase cultural system has long offered anthropology a way to name the organized life of symbols without reducing meaning to either private psychology or social function. Yet the phrase also hides a practical difficulty: symbols, rules, classifications, schedules, offices, and models do not carry their consequences inside themselves. They must be taken up by people, artifacts, institutions, bodies, clocks, infrastructures, and trained capacities; they must meet situations for which they are never fully specified; and they must survive disagreement, failure, resistance, and repair. Beginning with Clifford Geertz's 1957 account of a Javanese funeral that would not proceed, this essay follows that difficulty through his writings on religion, ideology, common sense, art, law, and cultural "programs," then outward through rival theories of cultural systems, distributed cognition, situated action, classification, infrastructure, ritual ecology, media, temporality, and repair. I use the term cultural runtime not as a claim that culture is software, but as a deliberately awkward name for the arrangements through which symbolic forms acquire a practical career. The wager is that interpretive anthropology becomes sharper, not less interpretive, when it asks not only what signs mean but what lets them get to work.
keywords: [Clifford Geertz, cultural systems, interpretation, cultural runtime, distributed cognition, situated action, infrastructure, repair]

A boy dies in Modjokuto, and a funeral - the sort of institution one might expect to know what to do when ordinary life has been stopped by death - discovers that it does not. The boy is Paidjan. His family and neighbors possess no shortage of funeral knowledge. There are prayers to be said, offices to be occupied, sequences to be followed, a body to be buried, people who know what these things mean and, ordinarily, who does what next. Yet the Modin, the religious official upon whom part of the sequence depends, has been instructed not to conduct the rite for members of Permai, the political-religious organization with which Paidjan's family is associated. The ritual form remains available. The social arrangement that once let the form pass without incident into action does not. The funeral catches. [@geertz1957ritual]

Geertz's early essay on this event is usually remembered for the theoretical distinction he drew from it: culture and social structure are not merely two names for the same thing, and their modes of integration can become incongruent enough that a ritual whose forms are perfectly recognizable no longer integrates anything very well. But the event is more interesting if we resist turning it too quickly into an example. Everyone does not suddenly forget what a funeral is. The signs do not evaporate. The sequence has not become meaningless. A relation required to carry the sequence forward - between an office, an officeholder, a political instruction, a ritual obligation, and a bereaved family - has ceased to line up with the rest. The event becomes difficult not because the cultural form has disappeared but because the arrangements by which it ordinarily enters the world have shifted underneath it.

That small practical embarrassment is the point of departure here. The phrase *cultural system* has often helped anthropologists insist that symbols are not decorative surfaces pasted onto a more real social machinery. Yet the phrase can also make the machinery disappear by sounding as though systematicity were already established. What makes a cultural form systematic? Is it the internal fit of meanings, the repetition of use, the institutions that carry them, the people trained to perceive them, the artifacts that store them, the authority that licenses them, the schedules that order them, the resources that sustain them, or the capacity of the whole arrangement to move from one socially consequential state to another? And when these come apart, which one have we been calling the system?

I will use the term *runtime* for this problem. The word is ugly enough to prevent reverence. It is borrowed from computing, where a program on the page is not yet a running program; instructions acquire effects only inside an environment of memory, permissions, clocks, libraries, devices, processes, and inputs. Human life is not a computer, and cultural forms are not machine code. But the distinction between specification and execution is useful because it makes one ask an anthropological question that is easy to leave vague: through what concrete arrangements does a publicly available description acquire a practical career?

The answer, if there is one, will not be a hidden kernel underneath culture. It will be different in different cases. A funeral may require an authorized officeholder; a legal category a form and a clerk; a musical distinction an ear trained over years; a clock a wage relation; a diagnosis a clinic and an insurance code; a television text a receiver positioned to decode it; a boundary object a set of local translations; a repair a worker who knows where the official description stops being enough. The argument of this essay is therefore not that culture is software. It is that a cultural account becomes more exact when it distinguishes the symbolic form from the heterogeneous conditions under which that form can make a difference to what happens next.
