# II. Programs Outside the Skin

There is one reason the language of computation keeps returning to this discussion, and it is not entirely a contemporary imposition on Geertz. In his essay on the concept of man he described culture as consisting in "control mechanisms" - plans, recipes, rules, instructions, what computer engineers called programs - on which human beings, comparatively unfinished animals, depend for the ordering of conduct. [@geertz1973impact] In his earlier essay on culture and mind he had already emphasized the dependence of human thought upon publicly available symbolic resources rather than upon a self-sufficient interior apparatus. [@geertz1962growth]

The crucial word is not *program* but *outside*. A recipe is not inside the cook in the same way a reflex is inside a nervous system. A prayer, calendar, kin term, title, map, traffic sign, melody, theorem, story, office, legal form, or ritual sequence exists in a public world in which people can encounter, repeat, alter, contest, forget, restore, and hand it to one another. A cultural program, in Geertz's sense, is extragenetic not because it behaves like compiled code but because it supplies organized distinctions and directions from beyond the organism's biological inheritance.

This also explains why Craik matters. Geertz's famous distinction between "models of" and "models for" has a traceable precursor in Kenneth Craik's account of models that parallel external processes so that consequences can be anticipated. [@craik1943nature] Geertz's contribution is to make symbolic models public, social, and reversible. A dam can be made after a hydraulic theory just as a theory can be built from the flow of water. Symbolic forms are capable both of representing a world and of helping to organize one.

Yet to call them programs does not solve the problem of execution. Goodenough's almost contemporary definition of culture asks what one must know or believe in order to operate acceptably as a member of a society. [@goodenough1957cultural] This places cultural competence closer to the actor. Wallace then complicates even that picture by arguing for an "organization of diversity": coordinated social systems do not require identical cognitive maps in every participant. [@wallace1961culture] A court can work because the judge, clerk, attorney, witness, bailiff, record system, and defendant know different things and occupy different positions. An orchestra works because the conductor, violinist, stagehand, and audience do not share a complete representation of the performance. A hospital depends on differentiated ignorance as much as on shared knowledge.

Hutchins's study of navigation makes the point technically sharper. The computational work of fixing a ship's position is not merely performed by several individuals in parallel; it can be a property of the socially organized system of people, instruments, representations, and handoffs. [@hutchins1995cognition] Change the organization of the information flow and one changes what the system can compute, even if the individual people remain the same. What anthropology had often treated as context can become architecture.

The result is a cultural program with no single processor. Its state is distributed, its interpreters are partial, and its successful operation depends upon interfaces among nonidentical local models. Star and Griesemer's boundary object is a particularly elegant instance: an object can remain weakly structured in common use while becoming strongly structured within distinct local worlds. [@stargriesemer1989boundary] The museum specimen, map, form, standard, or category permits coordination not because everybody means the same thing by it but because it is stable enough to survive translation and plastic enough to be locally useful.

One might call this a cultural ABI, but the engineering joke is less important than the anthropological correction. Consensus is not the only way symbolic order holds together. A system can be integrated by compatible handoffs among people who disagree, know different things, or inhabit the common object at different levels of specificity. This matters for religion, law, science, bureaucracy, design, and every institution in which the appearance of one shared system is produced by many locally different practices.
