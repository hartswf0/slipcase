# IX. Repair, Tactics, and the Mutation of Order

Theories of culture have often been better at explaining reproduction than maintenance. Reproduction sounds orderly: a code is passed on, a value internalized, a ritual repeated. Maintenance is messier. It begins from drift.

Moore's work on law as process and the later "truing" vocabulary of design both point toward a view in which coherence is not given but continually achieved. Rules are regularized; heterogeneous elements are brought into tolerable alignment; discrepancies are managed rather than eliminated. Jackson's "broken world" perspective makes the point explicit: repair is not simply the restoration of a prior state but a site at which knowledge, care, power, and technological difference are produced. [@jackson2014rethinking]

A patched system remembers the failure. The new form includes another box. The institution adds an exception. The ritual acquires a variation. The machine receives a shim. The staff invent a workaround and later train newcomers in it as though it had always been there. From a distance the system looks continuous. Up close continuity is a sediment of repairs.

De Certeau reminds us that not every alteration is authorized from above. Users poach operations from systems designed by others, using official materials for unofficial purposes. [@decerteau1984practice] A tactic is especially revealing because it lacks a proper place of its own; it survives by timing, local knowledge, and opportunistic reuse. Some tactics vanish. Others stabilize, acquire names, spaces, budgets, and rules, and thereby become strategies or institutions. The boundary between use and redesign is historically porous.

Ortner's practice-theoretical turn makes the same problem unavoidable in cultural analysis. If actors reproduce systems only by acting within them, they also have opportunities to bend, evade, reinterpret, and alter them. The cultural system is therefore not a static environment surrounding practice. It is partly made in the practices whose possibilities it helps to organize.

Wilder's mathematics and Kuhn's paradigms carry mutation one level higher. [@wilder1981mathematics; @kuhn1962structure] Some innovations do not merely move the system into a new state; they alter what counts as a possible state or legitimate problem. Zero, complex numbers, non-Euclidean geometry, a new scientific paradigm, a new legal person, a new media form, a new interface primitive - these can reorganize the space within which later questions become askable. Cultural change sometimes modifies the rules by which change itself is recognized.

The lesson is not that culture is self-modifying code. It is that any serious account of cultural continuity needs an account of the processes that change the conditions of future interpretation and action. Repair, appropriation, resistance, and conceptual innovation belong inside the history of order rather than outside it as noise.
