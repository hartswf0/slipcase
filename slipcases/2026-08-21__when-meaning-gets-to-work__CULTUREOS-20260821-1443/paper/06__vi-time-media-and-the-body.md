# VI. Time, Media, and the Body

Some of the most powerful cultural arrangements look least like systems because they present themselves as conditions under which everything else occurs.

Time is the clearest case. Hall called it a core system of culture; Thompson showed historically how industrial time discipline reorganized the relation between work, measurement, morality, and habit. [@hall1983dance; @thompson1967time] When time becomes something that can be spent, saved, wasted, sold, billed, scheduled, and audited, a conceptual transformation has become institutional and bodily. Clocks alone do not create industrial discipline. Bells, wages, supervisors, schools, fines, factories, calendars, and moral vocabularies make clock time actionable.

The scheduler is therefore often more consequential than the doctrine. A school is partly a timetable; a factory a shift; a religion a ritual calendar; finance a settlement period; software a timeout and retry window. Temporal organization can coordinate, discipline, exclude, and produce expectations without making explicit arguments. It can also create tactical possibilities. De Certeau's tactic is defined partly by the absence of a proper place: the weak actor seizes a moment within terrain organized by someone else. [@decerteau1984practice] Timing becomes power precisely because spatial control is unavailable.

Media create a related difficulty of type. Peters's elemental account treats media as environments and containers of possibility rather than merely channels carrying messages. [@peters2015marvelous] Once media are understood this way, asking whether "media" are one more cultural system may be the wrong question. A medium can condition many systems without forming a bounded symbolic domain of its own.

Television makes this visible from another direction. The explicit Geertzian extension by Servaes and Lie treats television as a cultural system, but Hall's encoding/decoding model shows why transmission does not imply semantic identity. [@servaeslie1996television; @hall1973encoding] A technically successful broadcast can produce dominant, negotiated, and oppositional readings. The common message is not one meaning copied into many heads. It is an artifact circulating among differently positioned receivers.

Theater and dance complicate the picture further because the sign is often a trajectory rather than a stable object. Fischer-Lichte's theatrical system coordinates language, gesture, proxemics, costume, space, sound, lighting, and an audience whose presence is constitutive of the event. [@fischerluchte1992semiotics] Blacking's dance-as-cultural-system formulation makes the body itself a rule-governed representational medium. [@blacking1984dance] Yet Ingold warns against translating lived movement too quickly into a network of points and connectors. [@ingold2007lines] The route may not be a sequence of preexisting states. The line is made by moving.

This is one of the places where computational vocabulary becomes dangerous. State-transition notation can make movement comparable by chopping it into positions and thereby lose hesitation, flow, tempo, weight, continuity, and the very quality by which a gesture means. A useful runtime account must therefore know when not to discretize. The material and temporal conditions of a dance can be described without claiming that the dance is exhausted by those conditions.
