# IV. Things That Remember, Forms That Decide

The public character of culture has often been described as though publicity were enough. But public forms vary radically in the force with which they constrain action. A proverb and a turnstile are both available outside the skin. They are not available in the same way.

Akrich's account of technical objects begins from this difference. Designers inscribe scenarios into things: projected users, expected spaces, permissible sequences, imagined relations among actors. [@akrich1992description] The technical object does not merely tell a user what to do. It can arrange the environment so that some actions are easier, harder, or impossible. A form with a mandatory field, a gate that accepts one credential, a chair bolted to a floor, a software menu that offers three options, a courtroom whose furniture separates speakers - each carries a partial model of the world in material form.

Bowker and Star make the institutional consequences of such inscriptions impossible to ignore. Classification systems are tied to coding practices, standards, forms, databases, and work routines. [@bowkerstar1999sorting] A category does not become consequential merely because someone has named it. It becomes consequential when records require it, eligibility rules read it, bureaucracies exchange it, software validates it, and decisions are attached to it. The path from word to box to database to policy can give a loose distinction a rigidity it never possessed in conversation.

This is one reason the kinship, accounting, university, medicine, and law zettels matter together. Schneider's symbolic distinctions organize relations among persons, but institutional contexts can materialize relation types in documents and obligations. [@schneider1968american] Accounting turns representations into ledger states that later decisions treat as real enough to allocate resources. [@mouritsen1989accounting] The university repeatedly draws boundaries around knowledge through degrees, departments, review procedures, syllabi, rankings, and credentialing. [@considine2006university] Medicine couples illness meanings to clinical sectors, diagnostic pathways, professional roles, technologies, and treatment decisions. [@kleinman1980patients] Law turns the indeterminacy of events into the particular facts a case can recognize and then attaches decisions to those facts. [@geertz1983law]

The important distinction is between a representation that *can* be acted upon and a representation that is *wired into* an action pathway. A status written in a private notebook differs from the same status stored in the database queried by the gate. The semantic content might be identical. Its position in the arrangement is not.

This helps explain why classification can appear to make the world it only claims to record. Once people orient to the category, once resources or restrictions follow from it, once institutions expect it, the consequences of prior classification become inputs to later classification. The loop need not be total or deterministic to be real. A category may be contested, evaded, strategically used, or badly fitted. Its importance lies precisely in the practical career that follows the act of sorting.

The same analysis complicates Geertz's "models for." The model becomes a model *for* conduct not by metaphysical transition from representation to prescription but by being taken up within a set of social and material relations. A legal rule needs jurisdiction and an authorized office. A recipe needs ingredients, tools, and a cook. A clock needs institutions that care what time it is. A traffic sign needs roads, conventions, vehicles, enforcement, and trained perception. An interface needs a device, a user, a credential, and whatever infrastructure waits behind the button.

Meaning gets to work through arrangements.
