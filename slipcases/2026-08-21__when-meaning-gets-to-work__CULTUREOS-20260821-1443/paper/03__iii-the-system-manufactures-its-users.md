# III. The System Manufactures Its Users

A further difficulty follows. The participants capable of using a cultural system are not simply given to it. They have to be made.

Geertz's art essay is one of the clearest places to see this. A work is not culturally effective merely because a sign is present before an eye. The capacity to discriminate its form, technique, reference, and value is itself historically cultivated. [@geertz1976art] This is why the "period eye" mattered to his discussion: a society produces not only objects but habits of attention, perceptual repertoires, and criteria through which those objects can become significant.

Education makes the mechanism explicit. Peter McLaren's formulation of education as a cultural system and his analysis of schooling as ritual performance draw attention to the repetitive production of roles, gestures, categories, and authorized forms of participation. A school does not simply transmit propositions; it schedules bodies, grants access, evaluates performances, distributes recognition, and produces people who know how to move within its distinctions. The conservatory described in the music-as-cultural-system lineage does the same to the category "talent": the institution trains perception and performance while also evaluating the very capacities it has helped to cultivate. [@kingsbury1984music]

Lave and Wenger give us a better language than transmission for this process. Learning is a transformation of participation. [@lavewenger1991situated] A newcomer begins at the edge of a practice, gaining access to tasks, tools, persons, stories, permissions, and ways of seeing. What changes is not only the stock of information inside the learner but the learner's position in a social world. Competence is therefore partly relational: the same declarative knowledge can be impotent without the role, access, trust, equipment, or authorization that lets it count.

This point turns the old software metaphor inside out. An ordinary program assumes a processor already capable of executing its instructions. A cultural program often has to participate in the production of its own interpreter. The kinship system makes certain relations visible as kin relations; the conservatory makes certain differences audible; the legal profession makes certain occurrences visible as legally relevant facts; caregiving arrangements make dependency and obligation mutually interpretable; universities repeatedly mark what counts as knowledge and who is licensed to speak for it. The system's continuation depends on its capacity to make another generation of users for whom its distinctions are practically available.

Gender offers the most politically consequential mutation of this argument. Ortner and Whitehead explicitly adapted the Geertzian phrase "gender as a cultural system," then insisted that the symbolic system had to be grounded in social relations and practices rather than treated as an autonomous code. [@ortnerwhitehead1981sexual] The question of how a system produces interpreters therefore cannot be separated from who is positioned where, who acquires which capacities, who is authorized to perform which role, and whose actions can alter the system itself. Enculturation is not a neutral boot sequence. It is also a distribution of capabilities.

The cultural system that makes its users can therefore never be understood solely by listing its symbols. One must ask what forms of participation reproduce the competence to read them, what institutions grant or refuse access, what bodies are trained, what classifications follow the trainee, and what happens when users repurpose the capacities they have acquired. A system that produces interpreters also produces possible dissenters, improvisers, and defectors.
