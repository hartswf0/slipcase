Why Business Schools And Consulting Firms Are Adding So Little Value
By Steve Denning, Senior Contributor.
Nov 02, 2026, 10:02am EST

On October 18, 2026, Christopher Marquis, a professor at Cambridge Judge Business School, published a blunt essay in The New York Times titled “I Teach at a Business School. It’s Time to Shut Them Down.” The problem, he argued, is not a fixable misalignment but a matter of design. Business schools systematically promote deal-making, simplification, and optimization; they steer idealistic students toward consulting and finance; and the case method trains managers to view the firm from the vantage point of a single top executive, often on the basis of sanitized, company-approved narratives.

1. A Mindset Like a Virus That Has ‘Escaped Containment’

Marquis explained that the dominant mindset had “escaped containment” and, in a subsequent NPR Indicator podcast, clarified that it was like a virus. The mindset now shapes political leadership with a transactional view of public life. He cited research, including work by Nobel laureate economist Daron Acemoglu and colleagues, showing that in both the United States and Denmark, firms that appointed managers with business degrees “cut wages and reduced labor’s share of income” without corresponding gains in productivity or growth.

The essay has real strengths. It treats the issue as structural rather than a matter of adding another ethics elective, and it captures long-standing frustrations with career-office pressure, rankings that reward high starting salaries, and the gap between stated purpose and actual outcomes.

2. How the Author Modified His Message

The headline produced a strong reaction. In follow-up interviews with Poets & Quants and NPR’s The Indicator, Marquis explained that a New York Times editor had chosen the title He said he personally sought fundamental curriculum redesign rather than literal disappearance of the institutions—a curriculum that places long-term challenges at the core, and faculty incentives that reward documented real-world impact rather than primarily academic publishing. Even with that moderation, the article generated hundreds of messages, many supportive, and prompted responses from deans at Wharton, Oxford Saïd, INSEAD, and elsewhere. Most rejected abolition while acknowledging that reform was overdue.

3. What Marquis Missed: The Interlock with Consultancies

What Marquis and the ensuing debate largely missed is that business schools do not operate alone. They form one half of an interlocking system with the major strategy consultancies. The schools teach the familiar language of 20th-century management—hierarchies prioritizing profits, rigid processes, and related frameworks and metrics—that McKinsey, Bain, and BCG then package and sell.

Recent publications illustrate the continuity:
- McKinsey’s forthcoming Strategy and Value (Koller, West, and Zimmerman) is framed as the companion to the firm’s long-running valuation work. It aims to link strategy decisions to long-term economic profit, capital allocation, and portfolio choices, with (so far) no mention of the customer.
- Bain’s July–August 2026 Harvard Business Review article “Bring Back Managing for Value” and related pieces on strategic fit argue for a return to maximizing shareholder value—the very idea Jack Welch called “the dumbest idea in the world” and the Business Roundtable unanimously rejected in August 2019.
- BCG’s cover article in the same issue of HBR promoted “alignment” to the hierarchy as the key to the future—the essence of 20th-century management thinking.

4. Michael Porter’s Five Forces Remain a Business School Staple

None of these restatements treat continuous increases in customer value as the central strategic task. The omission is consequential. Michael Porter’s Five Forces, still a business-school staple, analyzes industry structure and the division of existing value. Bargaining power of buyers appears only as a force that can erode margins; there is no mechanism for systematically raising the value delivered to customers. Porter published no large-scale statistical evidence that systematic use of the framework produced superior firm performance. The issue is compounded by the fact that the strategy group he cofounded, The Monitor Group, filed for bankruptcy in 2012. Yet the tool remains standard.

5. What Marquis Also Missed: The Different Dynamic of Today’s High-Performing Firms

Meanwhile, a set of high-performing firms has operated for years on a different paradigm. NVIDIA under Jensen Huang, Microsoft after Satya Nadella, Amazon, Costco, Netflix, and ASML have prioritized customer enablement, long-term reinvestment, talent density with substantial autonomy, and metrics focused on customer outcomes. They treat strategy as an ongoing process of adjusting to what customers will value next, not as a fixed analysis of industry attractiveness or a mandate to maximize short-term earnings. Their results are public and sustained, with extraordinary 10-year total returns.

Business-school and consulting discourse continues to revolve around older oppositions—shareholder primacy versus stakeholder or sustainability add-ons, case-method limitations, career-office pressure—rather than systematically teaching why and how leading firms are so much more successful by thinking, speaking, acting, and operating differently.

6. Why the Interlocking Alliance Survives

Despite its backward-facing thinking, the alliance persists for interlocking reasons:
- Faculty promotion and status depend primarily on publications in top journals; redesigning core courses yields little credit.
- Rankings weight graduate starting salaries and placement into consulting and finance, the sectors that still value conventional tools.
- Accreditation processes make core-curriculum change slow.
- Consulting firms sell frameworks that are teachable, scalable, and already familiar to boards and executives trained in the same language.
- Executives continue to hire the firms for legitimacy and political cover (“nobody got fired for hiring McKinsey”), to obtain surge analytical capacity, proprietary benchmarks, and brand risk-reduction.
- The highest-performing customer-value firms often rely less on these consultancies for core strategy.

Incremental proposals—adding customer-value cases, adjusting faculty incentives, broadening ranking metrics—face the same structural obstacles that have limited prior reforms. More radical redesigns confront tenure systems, accreditation rules, and employer demand that still rewards the familiar tools.

7. Meanwhile, the Paradigm Shift in Business Is Well Under Way

How then can the change happen? As business schools and consultancies spend time trying to revive 20th-century ways of running companies, the top-performing firms are racing ahead in a different paradigm. They prioritize customer enablement, networks of competence, long-term reinvestment, and talent-density metrics focused on customer outcomes, creating trillions of dollars of value.

Unlike the consultancies and the business schools, these firms are not pursuing yet another one-dimensional idea or a five-step process based on a single firm’s experience. They are led by entrepreneurs who learn from each other while finding their own way and doing whatever is necessary for success. The approaches are not based on the experience of one firm in one country and one sector, or limited to tech. The paradigm shift draws on the experience of hundreds of companies in multiple countries and multiple sectors. While the ideas are very 21st-century, many have deep historical roots. They are robust because they are evidence-based. This is not about something that is going to happen. It is already happening.

8. In a Genuine Paradigm Shift, Resistance Is Ridiculous

The thinking has also begun to spread in business schools and consultancies, although more slowly. Even in the organizations mentioned above, despite inertia and resistance at the top, individuals and segments are already making the change and experiencing the gains.

Business schools showing the way include Northeastern University’s D’Amore-McKim School of Business, Worcester Polytechnic Institute, Florida Atlantic University’s Madden Center for Value Creation, Simon Fraser University, and parts of Harvard Business School and Columbia Business School. Individual professors and consultants pursuing the change are too numerous to list here, though some have been highlighted in prior articles and podcasts.

Although the leadership of business schools and consultancies has not yet generally recognized it, we are in the late stage of a paradigm shift. When paradigm shifts happen in a society, there is not much point arguing about them. Once the public knows, it demands change. This is what is now happening with the shift from 20th-century management to 21st-century value creation, as leading public firms deliver huge, sustained, and quantified gains and benefits for customers. New patterns of thinking, speaking, and acting in business are showing the way. Customers will accept nothing less.
