ZETTEL

ID:
CBX-006B-ENDORSEMENT-REGRESS

TITLE:
Asking whether I want my desire merely moves the authorship problem one level upward.

SOURCE:
Harry G. Frankfurt — “Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person” — 1971 — The Journal of Philosophy 68(1): 5–20; John Christman — “Autonomy and Personal History” — 1991.

PASSAGE:
[PARAPHRASE]
Frankfurt distinguishes first-order desires from desires concerning which desires should become one’s effective will. Christman later argues that hierarchical accounts of this kind remain vulnerable because identification, higher-order desire, or even an integrated motivational hierarchy can themselves be manipulated.

RESEARCH OBJECT:
Reflective endorsement does not terminate the provenance regress.

LOCAL MOVE:
Frankfurt shifts freedom away from merely getting what one wants toward a relation between first-order motivation and higher-order volition. Christman pressures the shift by asking how the higher-order structure itself arose.

SOURCE TERMS:
first-order desire
second-order desire
second-order volition
will
identification
hierarchy
self-government
manipulation

WHAT BECAME STRANGE:
The obvious repair to SAFF—adding “does the agent endorse D?”—does not solve “Who wrote the desire?” It produces a new question: who wrote the endorsement?

QUESTION:
What prevents an optimizer from targeting the level of reflection that an autonomy test uses to certify lower-level desires?

DEEPER QUESTION:
Is every finite hierarchy of reflective endorsement vulnerable to an adversary capable of modeling and modifying the next level?

MECHANISM:
D1 = desire to perform action
→ D2 = desire that D1 become effective
→ reflective endorsement
→ apparent autonomous will.

But:

persuasive intervention
→ alteration of D2
→ endorsement of D1
→ certification of the intervention’s own result.

FORMAL SHIFT:
<FIRST-ORDER DESIRE>
→ <HIGHER-ORDER ENDORSEMENT>
→ [CERTIFY AS ONE’S WILL]
→ <APPARENT AUTONOMY>

SOURCE FORMALISM:
Frankfurt distinguishes desires of the first order from desires of the second order and uses second-order volitions to characterize a person’s concern with which desire constitutes the will.

No source equation.

OUR FORMALIZATION:
[OUR FORMALIZATION — NOT SOURCE SYNTAX]

D0 = action-directed desire
D1 = preference over D0
D2 = preference over D1
...
Dn = certification level

If the persuasive system can intervene at Dn:

CERTIFY(D0 | D1...Dn)

does not establish independence from manipulation.

TENSION:
Higher-order reflection appears to deepen autonomy beyond simple desire satisfaction. Historical objections show that reflective hierarchy and manipulated formation are compatible.

MISSING:
A non-regressive stopping condition.

What makes one level authoritative rather than simply one more writable level?

BOUNDARY:
Frankfurt’s 1971 argument concerns freedom of the will and personhood, not AI persuasion. The application to adaptive persuasion is our extension.

CITATION TRAIL:
[[CBX-006-WHO-WROTE-THE-DESIRE]]
→ Frankfurt’s second-order volitions
→ Christman’s critique of hierarchical/integration accounts
→ regress of endorsement
→ historical or relational alternatives

TEST:
For every proposed reflective autonomy variable R_n, construct a case where the persuasive intervention modifies R_n while leaving the agent able to endorse the resulting lower-order desire.

Continue until a criterion appears that does not merely relocate the provenance problem.
