TITLE:
STUDENTS DO NOT “SHORT” PROFESSORS THROUGH EVALUATIONS; THE STRONGER MECHANISM IS A CLOSED FEEDBACK LOOP IN WHICH FACULTY CAN RAISE THE METRIC THAT LATER JUDGES THEM.

SOURCE:
David A. Love and Matthew J. Kotchen — “Grades, Course Evaluations, and Academic Incentives” — 2010 — Eastern Economic Journal 36, 151–163.

SOURCE URL:
https://environment.yale.edu/kotchen/pubs/gradeval.pdf

PASSAGE:
[PARAPHRASE] Love and Kotchen model faculty who care about teaching but are evaluated partly through research output and course evaluations. In their model, placing more institutional weight on evaluations can induce professors to relax grading standards; under some conditions it can even reduce teaching effort.

RESEARCH OBJECT:
The relevant financial analogy is not short-selling.

It is Goodhart-like metric endogeneity:
the evaluated actor controls an input that affects the evaluator.

LOCAL MOVE:
The paper links institutional promotion criteria, professor grading decisions, student effort, and student evaluations in one incentive model.

SOURCE TERMS:

WHAT BECAME STRANGE:
The professor and student do not occupy opposite sides of a wager.

They occupy opposite ends of a circular control system.

The institution says:
“student evaluation measures teaching.”

The professor can partly alter that evaluation through grading.

The measurement channel therefore enters the behavior it supposedly measures.

QUESTION:
When the target of an evaluation can alter the conditions producing that evaluation, is the resulting number still an observation—or has it become a negotiated equilibrium?

DEEPER QUESTION:
Could grade inflation be produced not by students purchasing grades but by institutions coupling two otherwise distinct signals: student satisfaction and faculty career evaluation?

MECHANISM:
institution weights evaluations
→ professor anticipates evaluation consequences
→ grading standard changes
→ student welfare / effort changes
→ evaluation changes
→ promotion signal changes

SOURCE FORMALISM:
The source supplies a theoretical model containing:
- professor grading choice,
- professor teaching effort,
- student study time,
- institutional weight on teaching evaluations,
- institutional weight on research.

Comparative statics show conditions under which greater weight on course evaluations increases grading leniency and can reduce teaching effort.

The evaluator is inside the evaluated system.

TENSION:
Calling evaluations “options contracts” suggests students incur market-like positions and payoffs.

Love and Kotchen require no such mechanism.

The perverse effect arises simply because incentives become coupled.

MISSING:
Strong causal evidence establishing the magnitude of this mechanism across contemporary institutions and employment categories.

BOUNDARY:
This is a theoretical model. It identifies possible equilibrium responses; it does not prove that every professor inflates grades in response to evaluations or that student evaluations are the primary cause of grade inflation.

TEST:
Within an institution, exploit a policy change that sharply increases or decreases the weight of SETs in promotion.

Measure subsequent changes in:
grade distributions,
course workload,
teaching effort,
student learning,
and evaluations.
