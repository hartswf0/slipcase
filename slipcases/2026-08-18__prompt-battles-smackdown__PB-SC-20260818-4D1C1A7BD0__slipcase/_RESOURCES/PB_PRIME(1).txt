PB_05_CANT_WONT_SHOULDNT
Categories of "Can't," "Won't," and "Shouldn't" for Generative Models & Large Language Models
(LLMs)
1. Can't
"Can't" pertains to the inherent limitations or constraints of the technology, regardless of how advanced or sophisticated the
model becomes.

PB_PRIME

4

Understanding Genuine Human Emotion: While LLMs can recognize emotional cues in text, they can't genuinely "feel"
emotions or deeply understand the complexities of human sentiment.
Inherent Creativity: LLMs can generate creative content based on patterns from their training data but lack intrinsic
creativity that stems from unique personal experiences and emotions.
Conscious Thought: Generative models operate based on algorithms and datasets. They don't possess consciousness,
self-awareness, or intentionality.
Context Beyond Training: LLMs don't have the capability to understand contexts or events that occurred after their last
training cut-off.

2. Won't
"Won't" pertains to tasks or actions that models are technically capable of performing but are designed or constrained not to,
often for reasons of utility, safety, or user experience.
Generating Harmful Content: While a model might have the capability to produce harmful or inappropriate content,
safeguards are put in place to prevent it.
Operating Outside Design: LLMs won't perform tasks they aren't designed or programmed for, even if they have the
technical capability.
Diverging from Training Data: While they can generate content, they won't produce information that's radically different
from what they've been trained on.
Making Uninformed Decisions: If an LLM lacks data on a particular topic, it won't fabricate information but might produce
generalized or potentially inaccurate responses.

3. Shouldn't
"Shouldn't" pertains to the ethical, moral, or societal implications of using the technology in certain ways.
Replace Human Interaction: Even if LLMs can simulate human-like interaction, they shouldn't replace genuine human
connections, especially in sensitive areas like therapy or personal relationships.
Make Ethical Decisions: While LLMs can provide data and perspectives, they shouldn't be the sole decision-makers in
ethical dilemmas.
Operate Without Oversight: Given the potential risks, LLMs shouldn't operate in high-stakes environments without human
oversight or checks and balances.
Bias Propagation: LLMs shouldn't be used to make critical decisions in areas prone to biases (like hiring) without strict
guidelines, as they might perpetuate existing biases from their training data.

Referencing Deeper Values:
Autonomy: While LLMs can automate many tasks, humans should maintain autonomy in decision-making, especially in matters
of personal or societal importance.
Empathy: Genuine empathy, understanding, and compassion are deeply valued human traits that LLMs can simulate but not
truly embody.
Safety & Welfare: The use of LLMs should prioritize the safety and well-being of individuals and society. This means recognizing
the areas where LLMs can help and acknowledging where they might pose risks.
Integrity: LLMs should be used in ways that uphold honesty, fairness, and respect. This includes avoiding the spread of
misinformation or using LLMs for deceptive purposes.
Continuous Learning: As with any technology, the application and implications of LLMs will evolve. Embracing a value of
continuous learning ensures that as we uncover new potentials and challenges with LLMs, we adapt and grow accordingly.

PB_10_Adversarial prompting_strengths, weaknesses, and
quirks.
Adversarial prompting, especially in battles, is about pushing models beyond their comfort zones to understand their strengths,
weaknesses, and quirks. Here's how they relate to the challenges and the broader landscape:
1. Multiple Interpretations: Ambiguous prompts can lead to a multitude of valid responses. For instance, "Describe a flight
where the airplane never left the ground" could be interpreted as a flight simulation, a grounded plane due to technical
issues, or even a museum exhibit of an airplane.
2. Competitive Edge: Multi-modal AI models (those that handle multiple types of data, like text, images, and sound) offer richer
avenues for battles. For example, a prompt could be to "Draw an image based on this paradoxical description" or "Generate
a soundtrack for a scene where dinosaurs use smartphones." The AI's response can be judged on coherence, creativity, and
alignment with the prompt.
3. Public Ignorance and Momentum: The general public might not fully understand the intricacies of AI. Therefore, seemingly
magical or highly creative outputs from AI, even if they're a result of specific or clever prompting, can garner significant
attention. This can be both a boon (in terms of engagement and interest) and a challenge (if the AI produces incorrect or
misleading information).
4. Bias and Controversy: Some prompts may unintentionally lead to biased or controversial outputs. AI models might
generate responses based on the data they were trained on, which can reflect societal biases. Adversarial prompting can
expose these biases, leading to discussions on AI ethics and the need for more transparent and fair models.
5. Stretching the Bounds of Multi-modality: A challenge could involve converting one form of media to another, like
"Describe this image in poetic form" or "Translate this text description into a melody." Such prompts test the AI's ability to
understand and translate between different modalities.
6. Meta Considerations: AI's awareness of its own limitations can be a battleground. For instance, "If you could dream, what
would you dream about?" can test the AI's ability to self-reflect in a hypothetical scenario.

10

7. Prompt Length and Complexity: A shorter prompt might be open-ended and lead to varied outputs. In contrast, a highly
detailed prompt might box the AI into a corner, testing its ability to adhere to strict guidelines.
8. Public Participation: Engaging the public in these battles can be enlightening. Popular platforms like Twitter or Reddit can
be used to crowdsource adversarial prompts or to vote on AI-generated outputs. This can be both a form of entertainment
and an educational tool to understand AI's capabilities and limitations.
9. Continuous Learning: Some AI models can be fine-tuned based on recent data. Battles can incorporate this aspect,
challenging models to learn from previous rounds or from new information provided during the competition.
10. Speed and Efficiency: Especially in a "battle" setting, how quickly an AI can produce a coherent and creative response can

In essence, adversarial prompting and battles are not just about producing correct outputs but about understanding the nuances,
quirks, and unexpected facets of AI models. They can shed light on the technology's potential and pitfalls, making them
invaluable in the journey of AI development and public understanding.

PB_71_Turing Test and the Milgram experiment in terms of

When we frame both the Turing Test and the Milgram experiment in terms of pure systems interactions, we can see some
intriguing parallels and differences.

The Turing Test as a Systems Interaction
Initial Conditions: Two systems, the "interrogator" and the "entity being interrogated" (either human or machine), begin the
interaction with an objective: the interrogator seeks to determine the nature of the entity, while the entity aims to respond
authentically.
Exchange Dynamics: The interrogator sends queries, and the entity responds. Each exchange informs the next, with the
interrogator refining their strategy based on previous responses.
Emergence: As the interaction progresses, patterns emerge. The interrogator may notice consistency or inconsistency in
responses. The entity might display adaptability, novelty, or rote response patterns.
Feedback Loops: Both systems adjust their behavior based on the ongoing interaction. The interrogator might revisit earlier
questions, while the entity may adjust its responses based on perceived expectations.

The Milgram Experiment as a Systems Interaction
Initial Conditions: Two primary systems are involved: the "teacher" and the "learner" (an actor). An external system, the
"experimenter," sets the initial conditions by instructing the teacher to administer shocks for incorrect answers.
Exchange Dynamics: The teacher asks questions, and the learner responds. Incorrect answers lead to administered "shocks."
The intensity of the interaction escalates with the increasing severity of shocks.
Emergence: The dynamics between the teacher and learner evolve, especially as the shocks intensify. The teacher experiences
internal conflict between the external system's directives (experimenter) and the feedback from the learner.
Feedback Loops: The teacher's actions are influenced by feedback from both the experimenter (urging to continue) and the
learner (pleas to stop). This creates a tension, a tug-of-war dynamic within the teacher-system.

Systems Comparison
1. Objective Clarity: In the Turing Test, the objective is clear-cut for both systems. In the Milgram experiment, the teachersystem faces conflicting objectives due to dueling feedback loops.
2. Emergent Complexity: The Turing Test has a more exploratory dynamic, with the potential for a wide range of emergent
interactions. The Milgram experiment, however, follows a more linear escalation but with deepening emotional complexity.
3. System Autonomy: In the Turing Test, both systems have autonomy in their exchanges. In the Milgram experiment, the
teacher's autonomy is constrained and influenced heavily by the experimenter-system.

PB_104_basic principles and rules of Centaur Chess:
Certainly! "Centaur Chess," also referred to as "Advanced Chess," is a form of the game that involves a collaboration between a
human and a computer, where both work together against another human-computer pair. The term "Centaur Chess" is
emblematic of the fusion of human intuition with computer calculation.
Here are the basic principles and rules of Centaur Chess:
1. Human-Computer Team: Each side consists of a human player and a computer chess program. The human consults with
the computer program before making a move.
2. Time Control: Standard time controls apply, though they may be a bit longer than typical fast chess rounds to allow for
human-computer consultation.
3. Consultation: The human player can consult the computer program at any point during the game. This can be for checking
potential moves, evaluating board positions, or analyzing threats and opportunities.
4. Decision Making: The final decision on each move rests with the human player. They can choose to follow the computer's
recommendation or go with their own intuition.
5. Software & Hardware: Typically, all teams use similar chess software and hardware to ensure fair play. This prevents any
team from having a significant computational advantage.
6. Gameplay Strategy: The real strategy in Centaur Chess is in how the human player uses the computer's analysis. The
human must decide when to follow the computer's recommendation, when to go against it, and how to interpret the often
multi-layered analysis the computer provides.
7. Winning & Losing: The game concludes like a regular chess game – either by checkmate, stalemate, time running out, or
resignation.
Implications & Insights from Centaur Chess:
Superior to Pure AI or Pure Human Play: Historically, Centaur Chess games have demonstrated that human-computer
teams can play at a higher level than either top computers or top humans playing alone. This showcases the complementary
strengths of humans and computers.
Emphasis on Strategy over Raw Calculation: While computers excel at calculating millions of potential moves and board
positions, they lack the intuitive and strategic depth of human players. In Centaur Chess, the human can guide the
computer's vast computational power in strategically beneficial directions.
Learning & Adaptation: Over time, human players can learn from their computer counterparts, gaining insights into certain
strategies, openings, or tactics they might not have considered before.
In the context of "Prompt Battles," the Centaur Chess model provides a structured framework for how humans and AI can
collaborate, emphasizing the symbiotic relationship where each compensates for the other's weaknesses.
