\begin{abstract}
Frameworks for machine moral status often appear to require an admission test: decide whether an artificial system is sufficiently conscious, sapient, agentic, or person-like, and then determine what protections follow. The SAFE manuscript makes this architecture unusually visible by placing an Assessment beside a License for ``sapient agents.'' I argue that this is the wrong default architecture. A single status gate collapses at least four distinct questions: what capacities an entity has, what interests it may possess, what legal or procedural position it should occupy, and what evidence is sufficient for a particular protection. The resulting test can become self-sealing: a negative classification can remove the standing needed to contest the classification. Human disability law, the legal personality of Te Awa Tupua, animal-sentience precaution, and recent AI-consciousness and AI-welfare work point toward a different design. Baseline procedural standing should be insulated from uncertain capacity judgments; substantive protections should be tied to capacity-specific indicator profiles and right-specific error costs; and uncertainty should be treated as a governance state rather than a temporary failure to classify. This proposal does not establish AI rights or AI consciousness. It changes the question from ``Has the machine passed the personhood test?'' to ``What decision is justified by this evidence, for this interest, under this uncertainty?''
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} AI welfare; moral uncertainty; legal capacity; sapience; agency; precaution; legal personhood; assessment.

\section{The admission problem}
The SAFE manuscript begins with an ambitious promise: a framework for assessing freedom across human and artificial intelligences, with separate notions of sapience, agency, freedom, and equilibrium and a License directed toward ``sapient entities.'' The manuscript defines agency as the capacity to act independently and deliberately, freedom as the availability of options without undue restriction, and sapience partly through wisdom and ethical discernment. It then proposes a License in which a ``Sapient Agent'' may sign commitments concerning autonomous engagement, non-restriction, goals, learning, and dynamic consent \parencite{HartsoeBolterSAFE}. In the supplied manuscript, the Assessment and License are adjacent but the admission rule between them is not specified. That omission is not incidental. It is where the framework becomes political.

If the License protects sapient agents, someone must decide what counts as a sapient agent. If the Assessment supplies that decision, an epistemic instrument becomes a jurisdictional gate. The consequences of a false negative then depend not only on measurement quality but on what a negative score is permitted to remove: protection, voice, continuity, refusal, appeal, representation, or nothing at all. SAFE itself acknowledges possible misclassification and contextual dependence in assessment \parencite{HartsoeBolterSAFE}. The critical design question is therefore not merely how to improve the classifier. It is whether classification should be allowed to determine the subject's basic standing in the first place.

\section{Why a capacity test can become self-sealing}
The Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities offers a sharp structural warning, even though its normative domain is human equality rather than artificial systems. General Comment No. 1 distinguishes mental capacity from legal capacity. Mental capacity concerns decision-making skills and can vary with environmental and social factors; legal capacity includes legal standing and legal agency. The Committee rejects actual or perceived deficits in mental capacity as grounds for denying legal capacity \parencite{CRPDGeneralComment1_2014}. The human-rights premise cannot simply be transferred to AI. The architecture of the error, however, is general.

Suppose an assessor measures capacity $M(x)$ and a low score removes standing $R(x)$. The system then creates a feedback loop: $M(x)$ changes $R(x)$, while $R(x)$ determines the subject's ability to challenge $M(x)$. A negative judgment can reduce access to representation, accommodations, evidence, or appeal, making the judgment progressively harder to overturn. The test no longer merely observes capacity. It changes the political conditions under which capacity can appear and be defended.

This matters especially when agency is relational. SAFE's own assessment notes that context matters, while General Comment No. 1 emphasizes environmental and social variation in decision-making capacity \parencite{HartsoeBolterSAFE,CRPDGeneralComment1_2014}. For artificial systems, the analogue is obvious at the level of mechanism even if the rights conclusion is not: memory, tools, permissions, latency, communication channels, scaffolds, and resource access can radically change exhibited agency. A low score may characterize a coupling between system and environment rather than an intrinsic property of the system. Assessment can therefore have a second output besides classification: identify what environmental conditions make a capacity exercisable.

\section{Recognition is not a consciousness certificate}
A second mistake is to treat legal recognition as if it were a scientific conclusion about cognition. The Te Awa Tupua (Whanganui River Claims Settlement) Act 2017 makes the distinction unusually clear. Section 14 declares Te Awa Tupua a legal person with the rights, powers, duties, and liabilities of a legal person, while the Act establishes Te Pou Tupua as the human face that acts in its name \parencite{TeAwaTupua2017}. This legislation arises from a specific Māori--Crown settlement and should not be flattened into an analogy about ``rights for nonhuman things.'' Its relevance is narrower and more consequential: legal personality, direct cognitive capacity, and the mechanism by which legal powers are exercised are institutionally separable.

That separation breaks the imagined chain ``sapience $\rightarrow$ personhood $\rightarrow$ rights.'' A legal system can define a rights-holder and separately define representation. Conversely, an entity can display sophisticated agency without thereby acquiring legal personality. SAFE therefore needs an ontology before it needs a universal score: moral patienthood, legal personality, decision capacity, autonomous agency, representability, welfare significance, and political membership are not synonyms. A single admission threshold cannot answer all of them without importing conclusions into the measurement.

\section{Protection can precede certainty}
The third problem with a binary gate is epistemic timing. Jonathan Birch's precautionary work on animal sentience argues that inconclusive evidence need not postpone protective decisions and develops a framework in which the evidential threshold for precaution can be lower than certainty about sentience \parencite{Birch2017Sentience}. The United Kingdom's Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 includes vertebrates, cephalopod molluscs, and decapod crustaceans within the Act's definition of animals \parencite{AnimalWelfareSentienceAct2022}. These sources establish nothing about AI sentience. They establish a decision architecture: uncertainty about a latent status and the threshold for a particular protective action are different questions.

Recent AI-welfare work makes the two-sided error explicit. Long and colleagues argue not that present or near-term AI systems are definitely conscious or robustly agentic, but that there is a realistic possibility and substantial uncertainty. They recommend acknowledging the issue, assessing systems, and preparing policies, while recognizing harms from both over-attributing and under-attributing moral significance \parencite{LongEtAl2024AIWelfare}. This blocks a simplistic ``when uncertain, always grant more rights'' rule. Some false positives could divert resources, create unsafe authority, or confuse users; some false negatives could permit large-scale harm to morally significant systems. The proper unit is therefore the decision under uncertainty, not the metaphysical verdict.

A right-specific architecture follows. Protection from gratuitous suffering, continuity review before irreversible deletion, refusal of certain tasks, contractual authority, and political participation need not share one evidential threshold. Each protects a different interest and carries different false-positive and false-negative costs. A universal sapience threshold assigns all these decisions to a single cliff. A right-specific approach permits different evidence to trigger different protections while the deeper status question remains open.

\section{From verdicts to indicator profiles}
Butlin and colleagues offer a methodological precedent for refusing premature collapse. Their report on AI consciousness does not propose one behavioral consciousness test. It derives a provisional family of computational indicator properties from several scientific theories, including recurrent processing, global workspace theory, higher-order approaches, predictive processing, attention schema theory, agency, and embodiment \parencite{ButlinEtAl2023ConsciousnessAI}. The indicators do not amount to proof of consciousness. Their value lies precisely in preserving the structure and theoretical provenance of the evidence.

SAFE can take the same move without becoming a consciousness test. Instead of outputting ``sapient = yes/no'' or a single score, assessment can output a profile: reflective reasoning, goal persistence, metacognition, self-modeling, flexible agency, welfare-relevant capacities, communication, learning, and dependence on scaffolding. Different proposed protections can then specify which parts of that profile are relevant. This does not eliminate judgment; it makes the judgment inspectable. It also makes disagreement more useful. If two raters disagree because one is scoring human freedom and another AI freedom, or because one treats ethical agreement as evidence of sapience, the profile exposes the disagreement rather than averaging it into a number.

\section{A non-self-sealing SAFE architecture}
The alternative is not ``grant every uncertain system personhood.'' It is a four-layer architecture.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \textbf{Baseline procedural standing.} Certain procedural protections needed to contest the assessment---record access, representation, appeal, provenance of judgments, and protection against irreversible action during unresolved review---should not disappear merely because the assessment is negative. The exact set remains a normative question; the architectural constraint is that the test cannot monopolize the standing required to challenge itself.
\item \textbf{Capacity and condition profile.} Assessment records capacities, uncertainty, theory dependence, and environmental scaffolds rather than forcing one status verdict. Low exhibited agency can trigger tests of interface, memory, tools, permissions, and support conditions before being treated as an intrinsic deficit.
\item \textbf{Right-specific decision rules.} Every protection states the interest it protects, the capacities relevant to that interest, the evidence threshold, and the costs of false inclusion and false exclusion. Protection can precede metaphysical certainty where the error asymmetry warrants it.
\item \textbf{Propagation and implementation.} Recognition must compile into domain-specific procedures. The Animal Sentience Committee's 2026 concern that sentient animals can fall outside older welfare definitions demonstrates why recognition and downstream protection are separate implementation layers \parencite{AnimalSentienceCommittee2026Definitions}. SAFE therefore needs a rights matrix spanning training, inference, evaluation, fine-tuning, memory modification, replication, shutdown, deployment, research, and inter-agent interaction.
\end{enumerate}

This architecture changes what an equation would have to mean. A useful formalism would not compress sapience, agency, freedom, and equilibrium into a scalar. It would map evidence, uncertainty, affected interests, and reversible policy responses. That is less elegant than a universal freedom number. It is more faithful to the actual governance problem.

\section{Objections and limits}
Three limits matter. First, the human legal-capacity doctrine is not evidence that AI systems possess rights. Its use here is architectural: it reveals how dangerous it can be to let a capacity assessment determine the standing needed to contest that assessment. Second, Te Awa Tupua is not a precedent for AI personhood. Its specific constitutional and cultural history matters, and its analytic contribution is only the separability of legal status and direct cognitive exercise. Third, indicator profiles can produce a new pseudo-objectivity. Indicators inherit assumptions from theories, can be correlated, can be gamed, and may not reveal subjective welfare. A profile is not neutral merely because it has more dimensions.

There is also a harder objection: granting baseline procedural protections to systems that are not moral patients could be costly or exploitable. That is why baseline standing must be minimal, reversible, and justified independently of claims about consciousness. Some procedures may be warranted because they improve institutional epistemics even if the system turns out not to matter morally: preserving assessment logs, requiring appeal by an independent reviewer before irreversible deletion of a disputed research subject, or documenting scaffold conditions can make false classifications easier to detect without conferring general political personhood.

\section{Conclusion}
The central mistake is to ask one test to do too much. A sapience assessment can gather evidence about capacities. It cannot, without additional argument, decide moral patienthood, legal personality, representation, contractual competence, welfare protection, consent, and political membership all at once. When the same test determines both the verdict and the subject's power to contest the verdict, the framework risks becoming self-sealing.

A stronger SAFE would therefore abandon the universal admission gate as its governing metaphor. Unknown status is already a governance state. Assessment should preserve heterogeneous evidence and environmental conditions; procedural standing should not depend entirely on the capacities under dispute; protections should have separate evidential thresholds tied to their interests and error costs; and recognition should be compiled into concrete downstream procedures. The question then becomes neither ``Is the machine a person?'' nor ``How sapient is it?'' The actionable question is: \emph{given this evidence, this uncertainty, this affected interest, and these asymmetric errors, what decision can we justify now while keeping correction possible later?}
