ZETTEL

ID:
BGS-1884-17

TITLE:
Thaler settles the identity of a sole machine author while leaving the human-machine boundary almost untouched

SOURCE:
U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit — Thaler v. Perlmutter — 2025 — No. 23-5233.

PASSAGE:
[PARAPHRASE]
The D.C. Circuit held that an AI system named as the sole author cannot qualify as an author under the Copyright Act. But the court expressly did not decide whether Thaler himself could be considered the author through making or using the system, because that argument had not been preserved before the agency. The opinion also describes disputes over how much AI may contribute to a human author’s work as line-drawing questions not presented by the case.

RESEARCH OBJECT:
The most consequential AI-authorship question survived Thaler.

Thaler answers:

CAN THE MACHINE ITSELF BE THE STATUTORY AUTHOR?

It does not answer:

WHEN HAS A HUMAN USING SUCH A MACHINE AUTHORED THE RESULT?

LOCAL MOVE:
The court confines its holding to the posture created by Thaler’s own registration claim: machine as sole author, human as owner.

WHAT BECAME STRANGE:
The most famous current AI-authorship case is structurally incapable of answering the question for most actual generative-art workflows.

Its clean holding depends on an unusually extreme factual claim:

the machine authored the work autonomously.

QUESTION:
What law actually governs the much harder middle where neither “human alone” nor “machine alone” describes the production history?

DEEPER QUESTION:
Has contemporary discourse mistaken a rule about eligible kinds of authors for a rule about sufficient acts of authorship?

MECHANISM:
Thaler’s registration position:
MACHINE = sole author
HUMAN = claimant / owner

Court:
machine cannot be statutory author
→ registration fails

Unresolved path:
HUMAN + AI TOOL + OPERATIONS
→ ? sufficient human authorship

OUR FORMALIZATION:
[OUR FORMALIZATION — NOT SOURCE SYNTAX]

Two independent gates:

GATE 1 — ELIGIBLE AUTHOR
Is claimant human?

GATE 2 — AUTHORSHIP RELATION
Did that human originate protectable expression in W?

Thaler strongly answers Gate 1.

It does not provide a general solution to Gate 2 for AI-assisted works.

TENSION:
The Copyright Office has already begun answering Gate 2 administratively through concepts such as expressive control, selection and arrangement, perceptible human-authored inputs, and modification.

The appellate case most often invoked in the debate expressly leaves those line-drawing questions aside.

MISSING:
Appellate precedent applying the human-authorship requirement to a genuinely mixed generative workflow where a human claims authorship and submits a rich production record.

BOUNDARY:
Thaler should not be cited as holding that works made with generative AI cannot be copyrighted. The opinion itself distinguishes works made by humans using AI and confines its decision to a machine asserted to be the sole author.

Search federal cases after Thaler for a claimant who:

1. identifies a human author,
2. discloses generative-AI use,
3. claims copyright in at least part of the generated expression,
4. supplies evidence of iterative human control,
5. receives an adjudication on sufficiency rather than sole-machine authorship.
