ENCOUNTER-001

TITLE:
The adversarial root of “encounter” does not establish an adversarial theory of encounter.

SOURCE:
Oxford Advanced Learner’s Dictionary — “encounter” — current historical note; Middle English origin. ([oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/us/definition/english/encounter_1?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

PASSAGE:
[QUOTE] Earliest senses include “meet as an adversary” and “a meeting of adversaries.” ([oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/us/definition/english/encounter_1?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

RESEARCH OBJECT:
The difference between a word’s semantic ancestry and the genealogy of a concept.

LOCAL MOVE:
The dictionary traces Middle English encountren through Old French encontrer/encontre to Latin contra, “against.”

SOURCE TERMS:
encounter
adversary
against
meet
come across

WHAT BECAME STRANGE:
The tempting claim that encounter essentially means confrontation may itself be a retrospective construction. The ordinary English sense changes from adversarial meeting toward unexpected or contingent meeting. Nothing in the etymology establishes that Buber, Goffman, Deleuze, HCI, or AI researchers inherit a continuous “againstness.”

QUESTION:
When twentieth-century thinkers make encounter philosophically consequential, are they recovering an older adversarial semantic residue, independently constructing a new technical concept, or simply exploiting an ordinary word already transformed by centuries of use?

DEEPER QUESTION:
Can “encounter” have a conceptual genealogy at all, or do several unrelated technicalizations merely share one lexical carrier?

MECHANISM:
Semantic inheritance supplies possible connotations; later authors selectively reactivate, suppress, or ignore them.

TENSION:
The earlier hypothesis treated contra → confrontation → alterity/resistance as a continuous substrate. Goffman explicitly excludes some everyday senses of unexpectedness and trouble from his technical definition of encounter.

BOUNDARY:
