TITLE: MY DATA CAN GOVERN YOU: THE UNIT OF INFERENTIAL HARM IS NOT NECESSARILY THE DATA SUBJECT

SOURCE: Salomé Viljoen — “A Relational Theory of Data Governance” — Yale Law Journal 131(2), 573–654, 2021 — https://yalelawjournal.org/feature/a-relational-theory-of-data-governance

PASSAGE: [QUOTE] “Individualist data-subject rights cannot represent, let alone address, these population-level effects.”

RESEARCH OBJECT: The parent asks what the primary public harms of widespread biometric inference are and notes a devastating problem for consent: even when one person withholds information, companies may infer it from sufficiently similar people. Viljoen turns this from a pathological exception into the central structure of contemporary data production. Commercial data analysis is valuable precisely because information about some people can produce population-level knowledge applicable to other people.

LOCAL MOVE: Follow the parent from “inferences made about people like you can tell companies about people like you” and “no longer voluntary not to infer” into a theory in which the relevant informational relationship is population-level rather than individual.

SOURCE TERMS: data social relations; population-level insights; supraindividual legal interests; social informational harm; data as individual medium; data as democratic medium; collective governance

WHAT BECAME STRANGE: [OUR INFERENCE] Refusing to disclose a trait does not necessarily remove a person from an inferential system. If enough other people disclose correlated signals, their participation can alter what becomes knowable and actionable about the refuser.

QUESTION: If my biometric data improves an inference that will be applied to people who resemble me, whose privacy interest is implicated by my disclosure?

DEEPER QUESTION: Does inferential privacy require rights over information “about me,” or institutions capable of governing the collective production of categories, correlations, and population-level knowledge?

MECHANISM: Data are collected from many individuals → analysis identifies shared population features and relations → population-level insight becomes economically or institutionally actionable → that insight is applied to other people sharing those features, including people who did not contribute data or consent to the inference → benefits and harms propagate beyond the original data subjects.

FORMAL SHIFT: FROM: DATA SUBJECT → THEIR DATA → HARM TO THAT SUBJECT. TO: MANY DATA SUBJECTS → POPULATION RELATION → INFERENTIAL RULE → ACTION UPON MEMBERS / NONCONTRIBUTORS → DISTRIBUTED SOCIAL EFFECT.

SOURCE FORMALISM: Viljoen distinguishes “data as individual medium” from an alternative account of “data as democratic medium.” Her relational account treats population-level and supraindividual interests produced through data relations as requiring collective rather than exclusively individual forms of governance.

OUR FORMALIZATION: [OUR FORMALIZATION — NOT SOURCE SYNTAX] {DATA_A, DATA_B, DATA_C ...} → POPULATION MODEL P → INFERENCE RULE R → PERSON_X WHO SHARES FEATURES(P) → DECISION_X, EVEN IF DATA_X = WITHHELD

TENSION: The consent paradigm assumes that declining collection preserves an individual sphere of control. Relational inference makes that premise unstable: one person’s disclosure can change what institutions can infer about another. Yet eliminating relational inference altogether would also eliminate socially valuable population knowledge.

MISSING: A biometric-specific account of which collective institutions could legitimately authorize, prohibit, audit, or redistribute the benefits and harms of population-level inference; a method for identifying the relevant affected group before the inference itself constructs that group.

BOUNDARY: Viljoen develops a general legal and political theory of data governance in the U.S. digital economy. The source does not provide a biometric-specific regulatory regime, nor does relationality imply that every population-level inference is harmful or illegitimate.

TEST: Choose one biometric target such as emotion, reproductive status, sexuality, health risk, identity, or attention. Construct two groups: contributors whose biometric data enter the system and noncontributors whose data do not. Trace whether patterns learned from contributors enable classifications, interventions, prices, surveillance, or opportunities to be imposed on noncontributors. If harm remains possible after every noncontributor opts out, classify that residual harm as evidence for or against the need for a supraindividual governance model.
