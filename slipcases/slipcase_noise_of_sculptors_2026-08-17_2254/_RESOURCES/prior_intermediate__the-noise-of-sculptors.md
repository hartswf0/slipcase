# The Noise of Sculptors

## What Happens When the Sketch Arrives Finished

**Watson Hartsoe**  
**Working Paper - 17 August 2026**

### Abstract

The first thing Midjourney taught me was not how to prompt. It was how quickly an image could disappear.

In the fall of 2022, entering a Midjourney image-generation channel meant entering a flood: prompts, four-image grids, upscales, variations, jokes, failed experiments, professional concept art, grotesque accidents, copied phrases, and strangers talking past one another while the screen kept moving. In our field notes we called the place “chaotic,” “messy,” and “fast.” A participant remembered that work could be “washed away immediately”; in busy generation rooms, more than one hundred new images could appear in ten minutes. We later gave this world a neat title: *Sculptors of Noise*. The title now feels backward. Before anyone could sculpt the noise, there was the noise of sculptors.

This paper returns to that noise. It follows one early exchange with a professional creative director, Joshua, who had generated roughly 24,000 Midjourney images for a VR game. A phrase he found in his second week produced an architectural style he had never seen. He spent more than 10,000 generations exploring it and redirected the game’s art direction around the result. What changed his mind was not simply novelty. The design persuaded him because Midjourney showed it to him in a state that looked finished enough to judge. In a conventional process, he argued, the same direction would have died as a rough sketch.

That observation exposes a deeper change in creative work. Generative AI does not merely make more images or expand a possibility space. It changes the order of judgment and making. Traditional workflows often require creators to decide which ideas deserve expensive refinement while those ideas are still crude. Generative systems can reverse that sequence: render first, judge later. They make it cheap to bring unlikely ideas to premature finish. The result is not frictionless creativity. It is a new problem of selection inside abundance. The early Midjourney Discord made that problem visible in public: a teeming workshop where almost everything looked finished enough to matter and almost nothing remained still long enough to understand.

**Keywords:** Midjourney; generative AI; creative practice; prompting; design; iteration; Discord; experimentation; selection

## The first channel

The first thing I remember is losing the image.

I had typed a prompt into one of Midjourney’s Discord generation channels. The bot answered, but the answer did not stay where I had left it. Other people were prompting at the same time. Their images arrived above and below mine. Four-image grids stacked into one another. Someone asked for a dragon. Someone else asked for a building. Portraits appeared, then disappeared upward. Variations arrived before I had finished looking at the first result. The channel did not feel like a studio. It felt like standing beside a conveyor belt that had learned to dream.

You could scroll back. That was not the point. The point was that the interface taught a bodily lesson before it taught a technical one: **there was too much to see**.

Our original paper preserved a cleaner version of this sensation. We wrote that when we entered the community we found it “chaotic,” “messy,” and “fast.” Hematita said that in the channels for sharing work, an image could get “washed away immediately.” In the image-generation rooms, more than one hundred new images could appear within ten minutes. People made private threads simply so their own work would not vanish inside the “huge wave” of everyone else’s images. Later, advanced users complained about “walls of images” and moved again into more exclusive rooms. The interface was not merely busy. It continuously erased the local importance of whatever had just happened.

That was the research site.

The problem with writing about it four years later is that the mess now wants to become a theory. We know the words that came afterward: prompt engineering, human-AI collaboration, latent space, control, co-creation, creative support, generative workflow. Each phrase makes the scene easier to discuss and harder to remember.

The scene did not arrive as a concept.

It arrived as too many pictures.

We eventually called the paper *Sculptors of Noise*. Clarinet had given us the phrase. He said Midjourney users sculpted from noise the way another artist might sculpt from marble; prompts were chisels with force, angle, strength, specificity, and other ineffable properties. It was a good line. It defended human craft at a moment when people were already asking whether the machine had made the art by itself.

But the line begins after the difficult part.

“Sculptors of noise” imagines a person holding a tool against a material. The sculptor remains singular. The material waits. The metaphor gives the human a hand, an instrument, and an object of work.

Discord gave us none of that calm.

There were thousands of hands. The tools changed. The material answered back. Other people’s experiments entered your field of vision whether you wanted them or not. A useful phrase could circulate through the server before anyone knew why it worked. A bad result could be a mistake, a clue, a joke, a failure, or the beginning of somebody else’s project. A model update could make yesterday’s technique stop behaving like yesterday’s technique. Moderators could prevent a line of inquiry. Super-user rooms could hide another one. Professional users could hoard prompts. Newcomers could arrive expecting customer service while early users remembered something closer to people gathering around a newly discovered fire.

The phrase I want now is the reversal:

**the noise of sculptors.**

Not because the users were merely noisy. Because the collective act of trying to make sense of the machine produced its own weather: prompts, images, guesses, rituals, workarounds, arguments, status systems, private rooms, spreadsheets, conspiracies, repeated tests, screenshots, and stories about what the machine “liked.” The knowledge did not sit outside the noise. The knowledge grew inside it.

That difference matters because it changes what counts as the object of study. The prompt was never alone. The prompt was one small move inside a moving crowd.

And the strangest thing the crowd produced was not a perfect instruction.

It was a way to see an idea before deciding whether the idea deserved to live.

## The message from Joshua

On October 12, 2022, at 5:56 p.m., I sent a Discord message to a user named Joshua. I had been looking for professional creators who treated Midjourney less like a novelty generator and more like a material with peculiar faults.

I asked him:

> What types of bugs have you found that produce creativity?

Two minutes later he answered that the most common failures involved misinterpreting artists and cultural references. The misinterpretation mattered because it was more unique. He was already talking about error as a resource.

I tried to make the idea cleaner. I told him it sounded like steering away from the currents and biases in a dataset while still using those currents to move.

“yeah,” he replied. “tricky!”

Then he asked what I was studying.

The answer I gave him is more useful to me now than most of the language I later wrote around it. I said I was interested in how creative people respond to the precise particularities of AI output and interface affordances through previous knowledge; they make what they want not by imposing a complete plan, but by adapting and “going with the flow” to some extent.

That was close. It was also still too polite.

The real issue was that people were learning to work **before they knew what kind of work this was**.

Joshua had been using Midjourney since mid-June. He used it mostly for concept design on a VR game. He estimated that he had made about 24,000 images.

I asked whether he worked one concept through thousands of images or spread those images across thousands of concepts.

His answer broke the paper open.

He had found a phrase that seemed to invent a new architectural style. He did not know why. He guessed that perhaps an early shape in generation got washed out, Midjourney reinterpreted the residue as a surface, and “a bunch of conclusions follow.” Then he stopped himself: “Dunno tho.”

That uncertainty did not reduce the practical force of what he had found.

The phrase “blew me away,” he wrote. It solved important problems in the game. He started reworking the game’s art direction around it. More than 10,000 of his images came from that phrase and combinations around it. He called it a “magical prompt.”

He found it during his second week.

“After that everything changed hehe.”

That sentence is easy to sentimentalize. Do not.

The interesting fact is not that a creative person had a revelation. Creative people have revelations every day. The interesting fact is that a phrase emitted enough visual evidence, cheaply enough and quickly enough, to redirect a professional project before Joshua could explain why the phrase worked.

He did not understand the mechanism.

He trusted the consequence.

## The idea that would have died as a sketch

I asked Joshua what had been so convincing about the new direction.

He gave an answer that I have not been able to stop thinking about.

The studio had earlier art that vaguely resembled the style. They had moved away from it because it did not look good enough. The direction had already been seen, in other words. It had already been judged.

Then Midjourney returned something close to that discarded territory in a more resolved form.

Joshua wrote:

> “the only way it sold me is because it was finished.”

That sentence is the paper.

Not “AI expands the possibility space.”

Not “prompts allocate unresolved decisions.”

Not “users engage in vernacular system identification.”

Those claims can all be useful. None reaches the nerve.

**The machine changed when judgment happened.**

A conventional design process often begins with cheap representations. A thumbnail sketch costs less than a polished render. A rough model costs less than a finished environment. A storyboard costs less than a shot. The cheap representation exists partly so the creator can kill bad ideas before spending the time required to finish them.

That economy has a hidden assumption: the rough version of an idea tells us enough about the finished version to decide whether the idea deserves refinement.

Joshua found an exception big enough to reorganize a game.

The rough version had lied.

Or, more precisely, it had told the truth at the wrong resolution.

The direction looked weak while crude. It looked persuasive when rendered further. Under a conventional workflow, the studio had a rational reason to abandon it: why spend scarce labor finishing an idea that already looked like it would fail?

Joshua said exactly this. There exists, he argued, “a giant set of designs” that do not seem viable from the side of thumbnail sketches and can be found only through more finished exploration. Most people do not have time to push all those questionable ideas to a finished state. Even if they did, the act would seem absurd: **why finish what you already think will not work?**

Generative AI changes that calculation.

It can make the absurd experiment cheap.

That is a more consequential creative affordance than “faster ideation.” Faster ideation keeps the old order intact: imagine, sketch, select, refine. Joshua describes a reversal:

**imagine, render, then decide whether the idea was worth imagining.**

The distinction sounds small until you notice what traditional creative pipelines discard.

They do not discard only bad ideas.

They discard ideas whose value appears too late.

Every medium has thresholds beneath which an idea cannot yet show what it is. A thumbnail cannot prove atmosphere. A wireframe cannot prove delight. A rough animation cannot prove the force of a final performance. A blockout cannot prove the emotional weight of architecture. A sentence fragment cannot prove the paragraph it may become.

We use low-resolution forms because we must. They let us think cheaply. But cheap representations impose their own selection bias. They reward ideas that survive simplification.

Generative systems weaken that bias by lowering the price of apparent finish.

The new abundance is not simply more possible ideas.

It is more ideas allowed to survive long enough to become legible.

## Finished too soon

This is why the first Midjourney channel felt so strange.

It was full of things that had reached visual finish before they had earned social importance.

A novice could type a few words and receive something with lighting, composition, texture, atmospheric depth, and the visual authority of an image that appeared to have taken much longer. A professional could generate dozens of concept directions before lunch. A joke could arrive with the surface finish of an illustration. A dead end could look like a poster. A mistake could look expensive.

The old visual cue that said **somebody spent time on this** had begun to separate from the image.

That separation created the feed.

If almost every experiment can arrive with polish, polish stops filtering attention. The channel fills with premature finish. The user gains the power Joshua valued - the power to inspect ideas at a later stage of representation - and immediately inherits the opposite problem: **too many things now look worth inspecting**.

The abundance and the insight are the same event seen from different sides.

Joshua experienced it as access to designs that rough sketches would have killed.

A newcomer experienced it as their own image disappearing under one hundred others.

The platform had lowered the cost of visual completion without lowering the cost of human attention.

That mismatch is the noise.

This is where our original emphasis on control began to mislead us. We asked how users controlled the image. The harder problem was how users controlled their own attention once finished-looking possibilities became cheap.

The expert response was not one technique. It was the construction of smaller worlds.

People made private threads so their work would not vanish. Hematita built a smaller server because the main space moved too quickly. High-volume users clustered in gated rooms where they believed “arcane knowledge” circulated. Some of those rooms later filled up too and were described as “gentrified,” forcing advanced users toward still smaller groups. Spruder avoided general channels. MariusJuston built a spreadsheet that tracked thousands of artist styles. Ignite maintained his own spreadsheet of artists, styles, and nouns for randomized exploration. Joseph protected the prompt at the center of his professional project. Cody Boy called prompts “trade secrets.”

The public feed created abundance.

Expertise often began by building a dam.

## What a magical prompt really was

The phrase “magical prompt” tempts the wrong reading.

It sounds childish. It suggests superstition, incantation, a belief that particular words possess occult force. Our fieldwork did contain plenty of that. Users repeated terms like “trending on ArtStation,” “4K,” “Behance,” “photorealistic,” and “Unreal Engine.” People argued about prompt leakage and whether one generation contaminated another. Users built mountains, valleys, galaxies, and ghosts to explain behavior they could not inspect directly. Some prompt discoveries came from glitches and happy mistakes. Others came from careful trials.

But Joshua’s magical prompt was not valuable because a word was magic.

It was valuable because it opened a **repeatable neighborhood of consequences**.

He did not generate one astonishing image and stop. He generated more than ten thousand images around the phrase. He varied styles. He hit dead ends. When something showed progress, he iterated and honed in. He treated the phrase less like a spell than like a place he could keep returning to.

The magic was not certainty.

The magic was **yield**.

A good phrase produced enough interesting variation to sustain search.

This helps explain why the community’s folk language should not be cleaned away too quickly. “Mountain peak,” “valley,” “magic word,” “ghost in the machine,” “fuzzy model,” “thicker model”: none of these terms gives us an authoritative account of the underlying model. That was never their only job. They gave users handles for deciding what to try next.

A metaphor can be wrong about the machine and right about the next move.

That is why the distinction between craft and superstition becomes unstable inside a black box. If a user believes a false theory, changes a phrase because of it, and repeatedly finds useful outputs, the belief has failed as explanation but succeeded as an instrument. The practical question becomes brutal: **how much truth do you need in order to steer?**

The Discord answered with practice rather than doctrine.

Try it again.

Change one thing.

Run more variations.

Keep the strange one.

Ask someone who has made 25,000 images.

Move to a quieter room.

Do not tell everyone the phrase.

See what survives the next version.

This was knowledge before it had a stable language.

## The public rough draft

Midjourney made another strange choice that now feels almost impossible to recover as strange: it put much of the act of making in public.

Oscar gave us the cleanest description. Imagine, he said, using Photoshop with a setting that automatically published every autosave to your website and blasted each save onto social media. Nobody would choose that feature. In Midjourney, something like it was the default experience.

The comparison reveals more than a social affordance.

It reveals a new public object: **the rough draft that looks finished**.

Traditional creative communities often see finished work and retrospective explanation. The failed thumbnail stays on the desk. The abandoned layer stays in the file. The embarrassing intermediate version never enters the gallery.

In the Midjourney feed, intermediate attempts could arrive beside their prompts in public. A stranger could watch someone fail, revise, branch, upscale, copy, remix, and continue. The learning environment did not need to wait for a tutorial because the process leaked continuously into the room.

This was intolerable and productive for the same reason.

Your own work could disappear.

Someone else’s work could interrupt you.

That interruption could become your next idea.

Oscar described Midjourney as a “multiplayer Imagination game”: one person makes something, another person asks what else it could become, and people riff. The public stream did not simply distribute images. It distributed **unfinished trajectories**.

The feed taught by contamination.

This is another reason I no longer trust the clean metaphor of a sculptor standing alone before noise. The early Midjourney user did not simply carve a private block of randomness. Other people’s chisels kept entering the room.

The noise had authors.

## Learning to make a quiet place

Once the channel itself becomes part of the material, many behaviors that looked peripheral in our original paper become central.

The super-user clubs were not merely badges. They were attempts to reduce epistemic noise by sorting users according to generation count. The rule was crude: make enough images and unlock another room. Users believed those rooms contained more experienced people and more “arcane knowledge.” Predictably, some users learned to game the metric by running cheap, low-quality generations to increase their count.

The absurdity is instructive.

The platform measured expertise with volume.

Volume granted access to better conditions for acquiring expertise.

Users manufactured volume to enter those conditions.

The credential helped create the thing it claimed to recognize.

This pattern repeats across the community. Public channels created collective learning, then overloaded themselves. Private groups protected attention, then removed knowledge from the public pool. Prompt sharing accelerated craft, then threatened professional advantage. A phrase could function as communal knowledge in one room and proprietary capital in another.

The community kept solving noise by moving it.

That is what experts often do in a new medium. They do not begin with mastery of the whole machine. They construct a local patch where cause and effect become visible enough to work.

A spreadsheet is such a patch.

A private Discord is such a patch.

Ten thousand generations around one phrase are such a patch.

A hoarded prompt is such a patch.

A repeated face experiment is such a patch.

A metaphor can even be such a patch.

Craft grows where someone manages to make a small region of the mess answerable.

## The machine moved too

The local patch never stayed local for long because Midjourney itself kept changing.

Clarinet complained that users treated the beta like a mature product even while experimental parameters and behaviors changed around them. He said it was changing “literally every week.” This mattered because prompt knowledge had no guarantee of permanence. A phrase could remain identical while its effect moved underneath it.

That fact should have stopped us from treating prompt craft as a dictionary of good words.

A dictionary assumes a language whose meanings hold still long enough to record them.

Early prompt craft was closer to field biology during a storm.

The practitioners could discover a regularity. They could test it. They could teach it. They could write a guide. Then the environment could change.

This made the community’s apparent irrationality more understandable. When the machine is opaque and shifting, a failed replication does not tell you which thing failed. The original observation may have been random. The phrase may interact with another term. The seed may differ. The model may have changed. The interface may have changed. The user may have remembered the effect badly. The folk theory may be wrong. The folk theory may have been right last week.

Under those conditions, superstition is not the opposite of science.

It is one of the forms that incomplete science takes when the apparatus will not sit still.

The cure is not to sneer at the user.

The cure is to preserve the experiment.

## What should have been saved

We saved quotes.

We saved screenshots.

We saved descriptions of people and channels. We recorded more than one hundred hours of participant observation and more than fifteen hours of interviews. We were doing qualitative research, and we treated the community as a community.

I now wish we had saved something else with equal rigor: **the failed runs**.

Not because failures are morally important. Because they carry the causal shape of craft.

Joshua’s ten thousand images are not evidence that one phrase was brilliant. They are the terrain around the phrase. Somewhere inside that pile are the variations that went nowhere, the combinations that drifted, the outputs that almost worked, the moment an architectural direction started to become stable, and the point where the new style became convincing enough to change the game.

The finished asset cannot tell us that story.

The final prompt cannot tell us that story either.

If we want to understand prompt practice, the durable object is not the incantation. It is the **path of correction and selection**:

what was tried;

what appeared;

what surprised the user;

what the user rejected;

what they changed;

what they kept;

and when the thing on the screen finally became strong enough to change what they wanted.

That last moment matters most.

The output does not merely answer the prompt.

Sometimes it edits the ambition.

## The neglected thought

Here is the thought I think the old paper avoided because it was hard to make respectable:

**A great deal of creative judgment is not judgment of ideas. It is judgment of representations of ideas.**

We pretend the distinction is small because we have built professions around making it manageable. Designers learn to read sketches. Directors learn to read storyboards. Architects learn to read plans. Editors learn to hear a book inside an ugly draft. Expertise partly means seeing through an impoverished representation toward the thing it may become.

But nobody sees through perfectly.

Some ideas die because their early representation makes them look stupid.

Some people gain reputations for vision because they can tolerate that stage longer than others.

Some institutions select conservative work because conservative ideas are easier to recognize while crude.

Some genuinely strange ideas require too much finish before anyone can tell whether they are alive.

Joshua’s story makes that hidden filter visible.

Midjourney did not simply give him a new style.

It temporarily removed the need to believe in the style before seeing it.

That is a profound change in who gets to take creative risks.

The old workflow says:

**believe first, spend later, see last.**

The generative workflow can say:

**see first, then decide whether belief was warranted.**

There is danger in this. Cheap finish can seduce. A polished image can make a weak idea feel stronger than it is. Visual coherence can impersonate conceptual coherence. A model’s preferred aesthetics can turn exploration into a search through what the model already knows how to make look good. The feed can reward the instantly legible and bury the difficult. Premature finish can create premature conviction.

But that is not an argument for returning to scarcity.

It is the new craft problem.

When finish becomes cheap, judgment must become more expensive.

## The noise of sculptors

I still love Clarinet’s phrase.

“We are sculptors of noise” caught something true about agency. The people in that Discord were not pressing a button and accepting whatever came out. They varied language, repeated trials, shared tricks, guarded tricks, learned model tendencies, developed protocols, built communities, and used outputs to make decisions in the world.

But the reversed phrase catches what I had not yet learned to see.

**The noise of sculptors** was the condition that made the craft possible.

The flood exposed people to more experiments than they could have performed alone. It also made attention scarce. Public prompting created apprenticeship and appropriation at once. Cheap generations created more exploratory freedom and more junk at once. Model opacity encouraged bad theories and useful heuristics at once. Rapid updates made expertise possible and perishable at once. The same system that let Joshua take a discarded design to a persuasive level of finish made a newcomer’s image disappear before they could find it again.

This is not a balanced list of pros and cons.

It is one mechanism.

**Generative AI moves the bottleneck from making possibilities to judging among them.**

That is why the first channel matters more than the polished quote.

The channel showed the cost immediately. Once the machine could finish an experiment quickly, the scarce resource became the human ability to notice, compare, remember, and care.

Joshua found a way through that flood. He did not ask the model for one correct design. He found a phrase with enough generative yield to keep exploring. Then he spent ten thousand images learning what lived around it. The phrase mattered because it made a discarded possibility visible at the level where its value could finally be judged.

The magic word was never the word.

The magic was that the sketch arrived finished enough to argue back.

And once that became cheap, creativity acquired a new burden:

**we had to learn what not to throw away.**
