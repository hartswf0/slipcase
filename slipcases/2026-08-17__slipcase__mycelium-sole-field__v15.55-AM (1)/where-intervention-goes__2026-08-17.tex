\title{\textbf{Where Intervention Goes When Teaching Recedes}\\[0.35em]
\large Infrastructure, Thresholds, and Self-Organized Learning}
\author{Watson Hartsoe}
\date{17 August 2026}

\begin{document}

\begin{center}
{\small\color{quietgray}Working paper}
\end{center}
\vspace{0.5em}

\begin{abstract}
Accounts of self-organized learning often describe pedagogy by subtraction: fewer explanations, fewer directives, less teacher control. The early Hole-in-the-Wall and later Self-Organised Learning Environment materials complicate that picture. When direct teaching recedes, intervention does not simply disappear. It can move into the configuration of the environment, the timing of new possibilities, the placement of curricular objectives, the social position of a mediator, and even the conditions under which a person is willing to enter the learning situation at all. This paper argues that ``minimal intervention'' is therefore better treated as a distribution problem than as a single quantity. The relevant question is not only how much a teacher says, but where agency over the learning field is located and how visible or alterable that agency is to learners. Reading the source record this way yields a concrete research program: distinguish answer supply from infrastructure, objective placement, temporal perturbation, epistemic role, and entry threshold; then vary these dimensions rather than comparing ``teacher'' with ``no teacher.'' A final speculative section uses the source's own cellular-automata analogy to distinguish novelty injection from attractor injection, while keeping that analogy explicitly provisional.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} self-organized learning; minimally invasive education; educational infrastructure; mediation; autonomy; affordance; pedagogy; learning environments.

\section{Where intervention goes}
The most interesting question raised by minimally invasive education is not whether a teacher can be removed. It is what happens to pedagogical agency when the most visible form of teaching---answering, explaining, directing---is reduced. In the available Hole-in-the-Wall and SOLE materials, the answer is not a vacuum. The learning situation remains arranged, maintained, timed, framed, and socially supported in ways that matter. Intervention changes address.

That change is easy to miss because ``intervention'' is usually imagined as something a teacher does directly to a learner. A teacher explains a procedure, corrects an error, assigns a task, or tells a student where to look. But a learning environment can also be altered upstream: a permission can be removed, a device can be shared rather than individualized, a curricular domain can be selected without being announced, a stalled group can be shown one new affordance, or an adult can enter as a questioner rather than an expert. Each operation changes what can happen next without necessarily supplying the next answer.

The argument of this paper is therefore narrow but consequential. The distinction between ``more'' and ``less'' intervention is too coarse for studying self-organized learning. A better account treats intervention as distributed across several layers of the system. Direct explanation may fall while infrastructural, temporal, curricular, relational, or threshold interventions remain active. This does not make learner autonomy illusory. It makes autonomy more precisely located: learners can exercise considerable freedom inside conditions that were not themselves freely chosen.

\section{The scalar error}
The SOLE Toolkit provides a useful starting point because it explicitly relocates the educator's work. Educators are described less as answer suppliers than as witnesses, supporters, and providers of structure; sessions are organized around a large question, collaborative investigation, and collective review \citep{mitra2015soletoolkit}. This is often read as a withdrawal of pedagogy. It can also be read as a change in pedagogical mechanism.

A scalar model of intervention would place conventional teaching at one end and self-organization at the other. The more the teacher withdraws, the more self-organization appears. But the source record contains too many operations that do not fit that line. An educator may say almost nothing while still choosing the question, deciding the duration, controlling the equipment, configuring the interface, observing the group, and determining when the session has ended. Those acts are not equivalent to lecturing. They are also not nothing.

The distinction matters methodologically. If all nonverbal or upstream design is treated as background, then a study can describe a learning environment as minimally invasive while leaving some of its most consequential constraints analytically invisible. The alternative is not to call every design choice ``teaching.'' It is to separate kinds of intervention and ask what state each one changes.

\section{Infrastructure: the teacher behind the wall}
The 2001 Hole-in-the-Wall account is unusually revealing because it records ordinary maintenance decisions alongside the claim that children received no conventional instruction. The kiosk was introduced without an announcement or lesson, but it was monitored; the computer could be controlled from inside the adjacent office; and researchers could intervene when they judged it necessary \citep{mitra2001children}. When children learned to shut the machine down, a registry setting was changed to prevent shutdown. When the desktop accumulated hundreds of shortcuts, those shortcuts were removed. The diary could still truthfully say that no instruction had been given, because no one had walked the children through the interface. Yet the environment was being actively governed.

This is the cleanest place to separate direct from infrastructural intervention. Direct intervention changes the learner's represented task by telling them what to do. Infrastructural intervention changes the learner's action space by altering what the environment permits, preserves, hides, or resets. From the learner's perspective, the second kind of intervention can look like a fact about the world rather than a decision made by another actor.

That asymmetry is important in digital environments. Permissions, defaults, home pages, persistence, software availability, network rules, reset policies, and administrative access are all capable of structuring exploration without appearing as pedagogical speech. The relevant empirical question is not whether such maintenance is legitimate; an experiment needs a functioning apparatus. The question is whether those interventions are recorded as part of the learning system or excluded from the description because they occur behind the interface.

The point is also not causal overreach. The available evidence does not show that preventing shutdown or clearing shortcuts caused the observed learning. It shows something more basic: ``no instruction'' and ``no governance'' are different conditions. Any theory that treats them as interchangeable loses information about how the environment was produced.

\section{Time and objectives: seed, saturation, hidden curriculum}
A second form of intervention appears not in the environment's configuration but in its timing. Mitra and Rana describe a stage in which exploration stops producing new discoveries. Children practice what they already know; novelty has saturated. At that point, the researchers introduce a new ``seed'' discovery, after which another spiral of self-instruction begins \citep{mitra2001children}.

The seed is a useful pedagogical object precisely because it is smaller than a lesson. It does not need to specify a complete path. Its function is to make a previously unreachable region of possibility perceptible. The source therefore suggests a punctuated model of self-organization: exploration can generate its own momentum, exhaust that momentum, and then be reopened by a carefully chosen perturbation.

This changes the intervention question again. The relevant variable is not simply whether an adult intervenes but \emph{when}, \emph{with what informational content}, and \emph{with what downstream consequences}. A full answer, a hint, an unexplained demonstration, and a contradiction might all count as interventions, but they do not do the same work. One can imagine measuring the quality of a seed by the amount of inquiry it generates beyond what the intervener supplied.

Mitra and Dangwal introduce a related but distinct relocation of control in their discussion of curriculum. They note that learners need not be told that the material they are exploring is curricular and propose that the curricular agenda can remain at the planning level while learning materials are designed to provoke curiosity \citep{mitra2010limits}. Here the objective has not disappeared. It has moved upstream.

That arrangement creates a form of local autonomy inside a preselected domain. Learners may choose routes, collaborators, explanations, and search strategies while a planner has already chosen the territory through which that freedom will operate. This is neither conventional assignment nor unrestricted exploration. It is better described as a separation between \emph{trajectory autonomy} and \emph{domain selection}.

The separation has both pedagogical promise and governance risk. Concealing the curricular label may protect exploration from the narrowing effects of an explicit target. It may also make the planner's objective less inspectable. The source does not resolve that tension. A useful experiment would compare explicit objectives, hidden objectives, learner-authored objectives, and genuinely open exploration while holding the underlying materials constant.

\section{Epistemic roles: encouragement without answer supply}
The Kalikuppam study moves intervention into yet another layer: the social distribution of expertise. The mediator was not a subject expert and was instructed to encourage rather than teach. The prompts attributed to this role are striking because they do more than withhold answers. They position the adult as someone who needs an explanation: ``How on earth did you figure that out?''; ``Please explain this in simple words to me.'' The authors report that the children responded by teaching her \citep{mitra2010limits}.

The operation here is not informational transfer from adult to child. It is a change in epistemic position. The adult authority figure enters without occupying the usual answer-giving role and instead creates a reason for the learner to explain, demonstrate, and defend what they know. A learner's partial understanding becomes public and revisable because someone important is asking them to make it intelligible.

This suggests that the irreducible adult contribution in a self-directed environment may sometimes be relational rather than curricular. Encouragement can sustain search; curiosity can be socially rewarded; explanation can reorganize knowledge; uncertainty can be made legitimate. None of these operations requires the mediator to supply the target proposition.

The distinction should not be romanticized. We do not know from this evidence whether the effective variable was genuine ignorance, performed ignorance, warmth, novelty, language competence, status reversal, or simply sustained attention. Nor should an adult's strategic self-presentation be treated as ethically neutral. The source does, however, establish that ``not teaching the subject'' leaves room for a dense set of social interventions that can affect persistence and explanation.

The 2001 account also complicates any assumption that self-organization eliminates hierarchy. The children are described as dividing into ``knows'' and ``know nots,'' with knowledge circulating through friendship and exchange \citep{mitra2001children}. That observation matters because equality is not the only way a peer system can remain generative. What matters may be the permeability of positions: whether today's novice can become tomorrow's explainer, whether expertise circulates, and whether social prestige rewards sharing rather than hoarding.

\section{Thresholds: the people who never enter}
The most consequential boundary may precede learning altogether. In the same early kiosk account, adults living alongside the installation did not engage with it in the way the children did. Adult women expressed uncertainty about the language and operation of the machine; one asked whether it would ``give us food.'' The researchers record that, despite persuasion, the women did not begin using the computer. The summary observations similarly distinguish adult nonuse from the children's impromptu classes and invented interface vocabulary \citep{mitra2001children}.

These observations are easy to treat as peripheral to a story about children's self-instruction. They may be more valuable as a limit case. The computer was technically available to both populations, yet it did not become the same practical object. For the children it could become a toy, puzzle, social surface, drawing tool, or prestige device. For an adult with different obligations, expectations, risks, or anticipated returns, the same machine could remain irrelevant or intimidating.

Self-organization therefore has an entry condition. Before a group can organize around a resource, someone must interpret uncertainty as worth approaching. Public access is not identical to practical invitation. A system can be radically open after entry and still be selective at the threshold.

The available study did not investigate adult nonuse as a primary research problem, so the causal possibilities remain open: language, literacy, gendered public space, time scarcity, fear of damage, social embarrassment, unfamiliarity, surveillance, or perceived utility could all matter. The important analytical point is simpler. Access should not be modeled as a binary property of the device. It is a relation between a technical object and a situated actor.

\section{From scalar to vector}
Taken together, these cases suggest replacing a single intervention scale with a vector of distinct operations. One possible working representation is:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.21\textwidth} p{0.66\textwidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Dimension} & \textbf{What changes} \\
\midrule
Answer supply & propositions, procedures, corrections, explanations \\
Field configuration & permissions, defaults, devices, grouping, persistence, visibility \\
Objective placement & who selects the domain or target and whether learners can see it \\
Temporal perturbation & when a seed, hint, anomaly, or new affordance enters \\
Epistemic role & who is positioned as knower, questioner, teacher, novice, or witness \\
Entry threshold & whether the environment becomes practically approachable at all \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

This is not source terminology; it is an analytical synthesis of the source record. Its value is that it produces different experiments. Instead of comparing ``teacher present'' with ``teacher absent,'' researchers can vary one dimension while holding others stable. Does removing backend intervention increase exploration or simply increase failure? Does a seed restart inquiry more effectively than a hint? Does a hidden objective broaden search or obscure governance? Does a mediator's epistemic stance change explanation quality? Which invitation conditions convert nonusers into participants?

The vector also clarifies the politics of autonomy. Learner freedom can be substantial in one dimension and narrow in another. A learner may choose a route while being unable to inspect the domain selection; may control the interface while being unable to alter system permissions; may have freedom after entry while facing a high threshold to enter. Calling the environment simply ``autonomous'' or ``controlled'' obscures these combinations.

The stronger formulation is therefore not that self-organization requires hidden control. It is that self-organization should be described at the level where organization actually occurs. Some organization is generated by learners. Some is inherited from infrastructure. Some is introduced through time. Some is carried by social roles. The empirical task is to make those layers separately visible.

\section{A strange stepping stone: novelty injection and attractor injection}
Mitra and Dangwal make an explicitly speculative move when they connect their educational intervention to a cellular-automata study. They report a result in which a self-organizing system presented with a ``vision of the future'' retains and later reproduces something of that image, then cautiously suggest an analogy to human learning \citep{mitra2010limits}. The underlying cellular-automata paper is identified as Mitra and Kumar's 2006 study \citep{mitra2006fractal}, but its technical body is not locally preserved in the evidence set used here. The analogy should therefore remain an analogy, not a cognitive mechanism.

Even so, the phrase ``vision of the future'' creates a useful design distinction. The seed described in the 2001 account appears to reopen exploration by revealing a new possibility. A future image could do something different: orient activity toward a possible state while leaving the path unspecified. These can be treated as two experimental operators:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Novelty injection}: reveal an affordance, anomaly, or possibility that expands the reachable search space without specifying a destination.
\item \textbf{Attractor injection}: reveal a possible future accomplishment that may concentrate exploration around a recognizable state while leaving the route open.
\end{itemize}

This is a hypothesis generated from a weak analogy rather than a finding extracted from the literature. That is precisely why it is useful as a stepping stone. It can be tested without requiring the analogy between cellular automata and human learning to be true. One group can be shown a novel affordance with no target state; another can be shown an example of a possible future accomplishment. If the first produces broader branching and the second produces stronger convergence, the distinction has experimental value even if the biological metaphor fails.

\section{Limitations and unresolved evidence}
This paper is a working argument assembled from a bounded collection, not an exhaustive review of self-organized learning or educational technology. The strongest evidence comes from two published articles and a later SOLE toolkit. Institutional afterlives, adjacent pedagogical traditions, and critical evaluations are underrepresented. The claims here should therefore be read as a conceptual reanalysis of a specific source cluster rather than as a general verdict on the field.

Several causal questions also remain unresolved. The presence of backend maintenance does not show that maintenance produced learning. The presence of seed interventions does not show that seeds are necessary in all self-organizing systems. The mediator study does not isolate encouragement, ignorance, status reversal, or attention. Adult nonuse is observational and underexplained. Hidden curricular objectives are proposed but not compared experimentally with explicit or learner-authored objectives.

The cellular-automata branch requires particular restraint. This paper relies on Mitra and Dangwal's own speculative description of the analogy. It does not reconstruct the formal mechanism in the 2006 paper. A future version should inspect that source directly before using its mathematics for anything beyond experimental inspiration.

Finally, the intervention vector is an analytical proposal created in this manuscript. It needs empirical refinement. Some dimensions may collapse; others may be missing. Its test is not elegance but whether it helps researchers design comparisons that the scalar language of ``minimal intervention'' would otherwise blur.

\section{Conclusion}
The strongest lesson in these sources is not that learning improves when teachers disappear. It is that the disappearance of direct teaching can make other forms of pedagogical agency easier to overlook. A computer can be unattended but administratively governed. A curriculum can be hidden at the planning layer. A group can be left to explore until an external seed reopens novelty. An adult can stop supplying answers and still reshape the distribution of epistemic authority. A resource can be publicly accessible while never becoming an invitation for some of the people standing beside it.

Self-organized learning is therefore better understood here as local autonomy inside a field of conditions than as the absence of intervention. The practical question is not simply whether intervention remains. It is where intervention moves, which state it changes, who can perceive it, who can alter it, and who never enters the system in the first place. That shift turns a philosophical dispute about control into a set of empirical questions about infrastructure, timing, objectives, roles, and thresholds.
