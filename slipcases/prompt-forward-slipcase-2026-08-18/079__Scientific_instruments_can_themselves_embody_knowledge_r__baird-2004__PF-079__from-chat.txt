ID: PF-079

TITLE:
Scientific instruments can themselves embody knowledge rather than merely subtract from human knowledge.

SOURCE:
Davis Baird — Thing Knowledge: A Philosophy of Scientific Instruments — 2004.

PASSAGE:
[PARAPHRASE] Baird argues that scientific instruments and material artifacts can themselves embody epistemically important knowledge rather than functioning only as neutral carriers of theory.

RESEARCH OBJECT:
Instrument contribution need not be zero-sum with researcher contribution.

LOCAL MOVE:
Baird gives the telescope analogy a stronger philosophical foundation: instruments can contribute epistemically.

SOURCE TERMS:
thing knowledge; instruments; epistemology; material products.

WHAT BECAME STRANGE:
If an instrument itself embodies knowledge, saying that it substantially shaped the result need not diminish the scientist.

QUESTION:
Could prompt systems be treated as epistemic artifacts rather than merely aids to human authors?

DEEPER QUESTION:
What would have to change in Baird's account to include a linguistic-computational instrument rather than a material device?

MECHANISM:
<designed instrument>
→ [intervention/measurement]
→ <epistemic result>

FORMAL SHIFT:
NONE

SOURCE FORMALISM:
NONE

OUR FORMALIZATION:
[OUR INFERENCE]
INSTRUMENT KNOWLEDGE + USER KNOWLEDGE can jointly produce inquiry.

TENSION:
Baird's central examples are material instruments; a prompt is not straightforwardly a “thing.”

MISSING:
A theory of executable symbolic instruments.

BOUNDARY:
Do not cite Baird as having theorized prompts.

CITATION TRAIL:
Epistemic instruments; scientific software; computational models.

TEST:
Compare prompts with scientific software and material instruments along reliability, calibration, intervention, and embodied-knowledge dimensions.
