DQ-01 — WHEN DID THE WORDS STOP BEING THE CAUSE?
Priority: 10/10 field disruption · 10/10 mechanistic consequence · 9/10 tractability

QUESTION:
Under what conditions is the language visible to the user actually the causal locus of a generative system's behavior?

WHY IS THIS STILL A QUESTION? 1 — OBJECT:
“The prompt” is not one object. User text, rewritten text, token sequence, embedding state, compiled demonstrations, and soft vectors can all occupy the role.

WHY IS THIS STILL A QUESTION? 2 — PIPELINE:
A visible prompt can be transformed before generation. Surface-level prompt/output correlation therefore cannot localize the effect.

WHY IS THIS STILL A QUESTION? 3 — DYNAMICS:
Even if a concept enters the conditioning process, it can lose influence during denoising. Initial interpretation and final control are separable.

WHY IS THIS STILL A QUESTION? 4 — STATE:
Seed and initial latent conditions can influence content, especially when semantic guidance is weak.

WHY IS THIS STILL A QUESTION? 5 — SELECTION:
Generated artifacts may be shaped more strongly by which candidate survives than by which sentence began the process.

WHAT ARE WE ASSUMING IS ONE THING?
“Prompt causality.”

SPLIT IT INTO:
surface-text effect
rewrite effect
representation effect
generation-dynamics effect
random-state effect
selection/evaluator effect.

WHAT IF CAUSAL DIRECTION IS REVERSED?
Prompt wording may sometimes be a retrospective explanation of a successful output rather than its dominant cause: users keep, rewrite, and narrate the prompt after seeing what worked.

WHAT WOULD MAKE BOTH CURRENT SIDES WRONG?
If no single stage has stable causal primacy and control is inherently interactional: the same sentence has different effects only because other stages configure what it can do.

DQ-02 — CAN A SPECIFICATION BECOME LESS CONTROLLING AS IT BECOMES MORE EXPLICIT?
Priority: 9/10 disruption · 9/10 consequence · 10/10 tractability

QUESTION:
What does “specify the behavior” mean in a system where adding a requirement can make that requirement—or another one—less likely to be obeyed?

WHY 1:
Natural-language requirements compete for model attention and instruction-following capacity.

WHY 2:
Some omitted requirements are supplied by defaults, making explicit text redundant.

WHY 3:
A requirement can be enforced through prose, examples, decoding constraints, external checks, or evaluators; these are not equivalent.

WHY 4:
A longer prompt changes the context in which every earlier clause is interpreted.

WHY 5:
The optimal amount of specification can be model- and task-relative rather than intrinsic to the task.

ASSUMED ONE THING:
Specification.

SPLIT:
stated requirement
effective requirement
defaulted behavior
formally enforced constraint
externally tested criterion.

REVERSED CAUSAL DIRECTION:
Rather than requirements controlling model behavior, model behavior may determine which requirements practitioners learn to state explicitly.

BOTH WRONG IF:
The relevant object is not amount of specification but allocation of requirements among different control channels.

A requirements matrix that systematically moves each requirement between prose, demonstration, formal constraint, and external evaluation while measuring individual compliance and cross-requirement interference.

IF ANSWERED:
It would replace “prompt completeness” with a theory of control allocation.


DQ-03 — WHAT LANGUAGE DOES THE MODEL HAVE?
Priority: 9/10 disruption · 9/10 conceptual consequence · 8/10 tractability

QUESTION:
If a human nonword can be model-significant, a valid word can be nearly inert, a Unicode character can carry a cultural direction, and continuous vectors can outperform prose, what exactly is the model's operative language?

WHY 1:
Human lexical categories do not align cleanly with tokenization.

WHY 2:
Tokenization does not by itself determine embedding geometry.

WHY 3:
Embedding geometry does not guarantee downstream causal influence.

WHY 4:
The same visible string can acquire different meanings across model families and versions.

WHY 5:
Soft prompts show that control can persist without human-readable linguistic units.

ASSUMED ONE THING:
Vocabulary.

SPLIT:
human lexicon
token vocabulary
embedding neighborhood
learned cross-modal association
effective control direction.

REVERSED CAUSAL DIRECTION:
Instead of words carrying meanings into the model, model behavior may teach users which arbitrary textual forms count as meaningful instructions in practice.

BOTH WRONG IF:
Prompting is best understood as navigation in a control manifold for which natural language supplies only an approximate coordinate system.

WHY 3:
Competing concepts can occupy overlapping spatial regions.

WHY 4:

WHY 5:
Default motifs may emerge late rather than exist as fixed fallback states.

ASSUMED ONE THING:
Understanding.

SPLIT:
recognition
representation

retention
causal efficacy
visible realization.

REVERSED CAUSAL DIRECTION:
What looks like “failure to understand” may be caused by downstream competition rather than upstream semantic absence.

BOTH WRONG IF:
Representation and generation are not serial stages but mutually changing processes.

DQ-05 — WHEN DOES RANDOMNESS BECOME A SEMANTIC ACTOR?
Priority: 8/10 disruption · 9/10 mechanism · 9/10 tractability

QUESTION:
Does weakening prompt guidance merely increase output variance, or does it transfer control to structured affordances of the initial random state?

WHY 1:
Seed choice changes recurring default motifs.

WHY 2:
Initial latent blocks can have reproducible content preferences relative to a trained generator.

WHY 3:
“Random” describes how a state is sampled, not how the trained model will respond to it.

WHY 4:
Prompt strength may modulate the marginal causal effect of initial state.

WHY 5:
Default images may therefore be interactions between semantic weakness and particular latent basins.

ASSUMED ONE THING:
Noise.

SPLIT:
sampling randomness
initial state
model-relative affordance
semantic output effect.

REVERSED CAUSAL DIRECTION:
Instead of the prompt determining an image and the seed adding variation, the seed may propose latent trajectories that the prompt only accepts, rejects, or weakly redirects.

BOTH WRONG IF:
Neither prompt nor seed has stable independent effects; only their interaction is meaningful.

DQ-06 — CAN A MEASUREMENT SYSTEM HAVE THE SAME PATHOLOGY AS THE SYSTEM IT MEASURES?
Priority: 8/10 disruption · 8/10 epistemic consequence · 8/10 tractability

QUESTION:
When a detector identifies outputs that are unusually similar across unrelated prompts, how do we know the convergence belongs to the generator rather than to the representation used to measure similarity?

WHY 1:
CLIP embeddings are learned, not neutral perceptual coordinates.

WHY 2:
Contrastive spaces can contain hubs.

WHY 3:
Clustering thresholds create boundaries.

WHY 4:
Human and embedding notions of similarity need not agree.

WHY 5:
The source phenomenon is itself defined relationally, so measurement geometry is constitutive of detection.

ASSUMED ONE THING:
Similarity.

SPLIT:
human visual similarity
task-semantic similarity
embedding proximity
hubness
cluster membership.

REVERSED CAUSAL DIRECTION:
A detector may make a family of images look like a natural category because its geometry preferentially brings them together.

BOTH WRONG IF:
Default-image recurrence is real but has no representation-independent boundary.

DQ-07 — WHAT FILLS THE PLACE OF A MISSING CULTURE?
Priority: 8/10 disruption · 10/10 social consequence · 7/10 tractability

QUESTION:
When a culturally specific or low-resource term loses control over generation, what visual priors occupy the resulting output space?

WHY 1:
A system compelled to output cannot express absence by leaving the image blank.

WHY 2:
Fallback motifs are recurrent rather than neutral.

WHY 3:
Script-level features can activate cultural stereotypes independently of intended lexical meaning.

WHY 4:
Training coverage is unknown in proprietary systems.

WHY 5:
“Bias” can enter not only through wrong depictions of a requested group but through substitution when the requested concept fails.

ASSUMED ONE THING:
Underrepresentation.

SPLIT:
missing association
weak conditioning
substitute prior
demographic substitution
aesthetic substitution
script-driven bias.

REVERSED CAUSAL DIRECTION:
What appears as a default aesthetic may be partly produced by which languages and concepts fail to exert control.

BOTH WRONG IF:
The relevant causal variable is not language-resource status but local embedding geometry and model-specific tokenization.

DQ-09 — WHAT KIND OF PROGRAM CONTAINS AN INTERPRETER INSIDE ITS SEMANTICS?

QUESTION:
How should a program be described when some transitions are formally defined but another transition means “ask a learned model to interpret this natural-language instruction”?

WHY 1:
LMQL provides formal control around model calls.

WHY 2:
The model-mediated transition remains probabilistic and model-relative.

WHY 3:
Changing model versions can change the meaning of the same prose fragment.

WHY 4:
Soft prompts show that linguistic interpretation is not necessary for control.

WHY 5:
Constraint satisfaction after generation is different from semantics before generation.

ASSUMED ONE THING:
Program semantics.

SPLIT:
defined transition
sampled transition
learned interpretation
constraint
test
delegation.

REVERSED CAUSAL DIRECTION:
Formal code may not be controlling language-model behavior so much as constructing a boundary inside which interpretive behavior is allowed to vary.

BOTH WRONG IF:
Prompted systems require an effect-like semantics that treats interpretation itself as a computational effect.

DQ-10 — CAN CREATIVE PROMPTING BE AN EXPERIMENT IF THE GOAL CHANGES WHEN THE RESULT APPEARS?

QUESTION:
How can prompt effects be studied causally when observing an output changes the criterion by which the next output will be judged?

WHY 1:
Creative goals are often discovered through outputs.

WHY 2:
ChainForge-style comparison assumes a question or criterion stable enough to compare variants.

WHY 3:
Selection and revision create path dependence.

WHY 4:
Serendipitous outputs can redefine success.

WHY 5:
Retrospective narratives may falsely stabilize what was wanted all along.

ASSUMED ONE THING:
Evaluation criterion.

SPLIT:
initial goal
current criterion
newly discovered possibility
selection rule
retrospective justification.

REVERSED CAUSAL DIRECTION:
The output can cause the prompt goal.

BOTH WRONG IF:
Creative prompt practice is a coupled preference-and-artifact dynamics rather than optimization toward a fixed target.
