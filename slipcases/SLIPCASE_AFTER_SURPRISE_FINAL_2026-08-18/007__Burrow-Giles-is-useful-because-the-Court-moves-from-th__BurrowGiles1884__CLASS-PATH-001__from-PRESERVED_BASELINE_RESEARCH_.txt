TITLE:
Burrow-Giles is useful because the Court moves from the camera class to a production path.

SOURCE:
Burrow-Giles Lithographic Co. v. Sarony — 111 U.S. 53 — U.S. Supreme Court — 1884.

PASSAGE:
[PARAPHRASE]
In evaluating Sarony’s photograph of Oscar Wilde, the Court relied on findings describing specific acts: posing Wilde, selecting and arranging costume and draperies, arranging the subject, disposing light and shade, and eliciting a desired expression.

RESEARCH OBJECT:
THE APPARATUS CLASS DID NOT RESOLVE THE CAUSAL QUESTION.

LOCAL MOVE:
The legal analysis moves outward from:

PHOTOGRAPH / CAMERA

toward:

WHAT THE CLAIMED AUTHOR ACTUALLY DID.

WHAT BECAME STRANGE:
The case does not decide whether “photography” as a class is authored.

It examines a particular path of interventions producing a particular photograph.

QUESTION:
Can the methodological move from apparatus category to production trace be separated from the nineteenth-century copyright doctrine surrounding it?

DEEPER QUESTION:
What would the analogous evidentiary trace look like for a probabilistic generative system where requested properties and realized properties can diverge?

TENSION:
Sarony concerns a photographic process in which several expressive variables were directly arranged before capture.

Generative systems may mediate intention less directly.

Therefore the case offers a methodological inheritance, not a technological equivalence.

MISSING:
A principled cross-medium vocabulary for intervention directness and controllability.

BOUNDARY:
The case supports:

CLASSIFICATION OF TOOL ≠ COMPLETE ACCOUNT OF HUMAN CONTRIBUTION.

It does not establish the copyright status of AI-generated material.
