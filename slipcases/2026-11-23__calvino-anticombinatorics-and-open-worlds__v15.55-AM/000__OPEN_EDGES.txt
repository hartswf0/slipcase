[CALVINO-001] The 1967 literary machine was supposed to rebel against its own rules.
QUESTION: Does contemporary generative AI fulfill Calvino's prediction if it produces novel artifacts without independently changing its generative conventions?
MISSING: What triggers dissatisfaction? What evaluates formal innovation? Who establishes the criterion for changing the generator?
TEST: Compare fixed-weight sampling, adaptive decoding, and systems that revise their own generative procedures. Determine which operation, if any, corresponds to Calvino's proposed machine.

[CALVINO-002] The reader was the decisive literary machine before the clinamen returned to the author.
QUESTION: What would it mean to evaluate an AI-generated work without treating either its human prompter or its computational producer as the unique location of creativity?
MISSING: An account of how reception feeds back into the construction, circulation, and revision of a work.
TEST: Read the 1967 and 1977/1981 formulations together. Track where each locates the operation that transforms a formally produced text into literature: generator, author, reader, or social context.

[CALVINO-003] Anticombinatorics meant explicit constraint construction, not simply choosing from many possibilities.
QUESTION: What would a modern generative model need to represent before it could perform Calvino's anticombinatorial operations rather than merely resemble their outcomes?
MISSING: A shared representation that permits both learned generation and verification of narrative state transitions.
TEST: Construct a small four-character narrative corpus. Compare free generation, probabilistic reranking, and explicit state-based constraint checking. Measure violations separately from aesthetic preferences.

[CALVINO-004] The foundational combinatorial calculation contains a numerical error.
QUESTION: Does the error matter only as arithmetic, or does it expose a broader ambiguity between the mathematical possibility space and the actual narrative problem?
MISSING: Evidence identifying the origin of the arithmetic mistake; a specification of when two assignments should count as the same narrative.
TEST: Reproduce the count, then calculate how it changes under explicit restrictions on reciprocal relations, character mortality, duplicated motives, and permutation equivalence. Compare the different meanings of "possible story."

[CALVINO-005] Calvino identified character transformation and then excluded it from the computation.
QUESTION: What if narrative possibility is not a fixed search space, but a space whose constraints are continually rewritten by the story's events?
MISSING: A representation for belief revision, learning, changing motives, transformations of relationships, and conflicting character perspectives.
TEST: Run the same twelve-action narrative under two conditions: fixed character compatibility and compatibility updated after every event. Compare which plots become newly possible and which contradictions disappear.

[CALVINO-006] The machine becomes a character in a story whose algorithm never included it.
QUESTION: Is the failure to include the investigator and programs a limitation of the algorithm, or the device that makes Calvino's story literary?
MISSING: An operational account of recursive representation, unreliable observation, and the causal effects of investigation.
TEST: Build the four-person model, then introduce the investigator's actions as events. Determine whether the original constraints can still describe the expanded narrative without adding a higher-order representational layer.

[CALVINO-007] A clinamen requires an available compliant alternative; random sampling does not.
QUESTION: Can probabilistic sampling count as a clinamen when the system has not identified a rule it could have followed but deliberately chose to break?
MISSING: A formalizable criterion for aesthetic purpose; evidence that the agent recognizes the constraint and the compliant alternative.
TEST: Produce three artifacts: a constrained text, a randomly perturbed text, and a deliberately modified constrained text whose compliant alternative is retained. Compare whether the modifications have intelligible aesthetic functions.

[CALVINO-008] In 1977, another story machine generated events by simulating a world instead of filtering completed plots.
QUESTION: Why compare contemporary generative AI chiefly with Calvino's anticombinatorics when earlier computational storytelling also explored world simulation and causal inference?
MISSING: Evidence of direct exchanges between Meehan and Calvino–Braffort. A controlled comparison of their actual programs.
TEST: Give TALE-SPIN-style world simulation and Calvino-style filtering identical characters, actions, and starting conditions. Compare what each can produce without adding new representations.

[CALVINO-009] A narrative can invent the world required by its desired events.
QUESTION: Could anticombinatorics operate backward, constructing the necessary world from an imagined event rather than selecting an event from an already defined world?
MISSING: A method for preserving global world consistency while allowing new settings and backstories to be introduced on demand.
TEST: Begin with three independently imagined scenes from one fictional city. Compare forward world simulation with backward construction of a shared city capable of containing all three scenes. Record required inventions, contradictions, and unresolved spatial relations.

[CALVINO-010] Diffusion does not eliminate impossible images; it learns how to transform noisy states.
QUESTION: What exactly is being reduced during diffusion sampling: uncertainty, entropy, noise, admissible possibilities, or distance from a learned distribution?
MISSING: An explicit representation of shared fictional locations, object identity, viewpoint, temporal continuity, and narrative event relations.
TEST: Generate multiple views of a purportedly shared place. Check spatial and semantic consistency independently of visual realism. Then introduce explicit world-state constraints and compare the results.

[CALVINO-011] Calvino's geometric structures are not necessarily generative constraints.
QUESTION: When does a compositional diagram function as an algorithm, and when does it function as an explanatory representation?
MISSING: Draft sequences showing when each diagram was established relative to the prose it organizes.
TEST: Recover the documented stages of a Calvino composition. Distinguish rules fixed before drafting from diagrams revised during drafting and explanations constructed afterward.

[CALVINO-012] Calvino had already imagined an inverse computational method before the burning house.
QUESTION: Is the most productive historical connection to generative AI not filtering candidate stories, but generating a hypothetical world whose contradictions reveal something about another world?
MISSING: A representation for comparing partially known spaces and distinguishing informative discrepancies from arbitrary invented details.
TEST: Construct multiple hypothetical maps from descriptions of the same labyrinth. Impose different impossibility constraints, compare the maps, and assess whether the resulting discrepancies provide useful spatial hypotheses.

[CALVINO-013] The supposedly objective narrative filters already encode judgments about believable people.
QUESTION: Who decides that a fictional action is impossible rather than merely improbable, unconventional, or aesthetically undesirable?
MISSING: An explicit provenance record for each constraint and a method for contesting or revising its justification.
TEST: Audit each original filter. Classify it as logical necessity, physical assumption, social expectation, genre convention, or aesthetic preference. Reverse one assumption at a time and inspect the resulting narratives.

[CALVINO-014] The surviving Calvino–Braffort project is not one artifact but several incompletely aligned artifacts.
QUESTION: Which of these artifacts should be treated as the primary evidence for the historical experiment's capabilities?
MISSING: Original source code, complete program listings, test inputs, output records, and precise execution conditions.
TEST: Build a documentary concordance matching every claim in "Prose et anticombinatoire" against the published story, surviving synopsis, 1977 proceedings, and Braffort's testimony. Mark each operation as proposed, programmed, demonstrated, or inferred.

[CALVINO-015] Contemporary models may relocate selection into training rather than eliminate it.
QUESTION: How much of contemporary computational selection occurs before generation, through data selection, annotation, preference modeling, and optimization?
MISSING: A provenance system connecting particular training judgments to particular output characteristics; a defensible account of artistic evaluation across different communities.
TEST: Compare base and preference-tuned models on the same constrained writing task. Measure conformity, variation, constraint violations, and human evaluations separately.

[CALVINO-016] An admissible event sequence still needs an explanation for why its characters act.
QUESTION: Can a narrative satisfy every logical compatibility constraint while remaining unintelligible as character-driven fiction?
MISSING: A general account of how genre, unreliable narration, thematic ambiguity, and literary surprise modify the desirability of explicit intention.
TEST: Generate narratives with identical event inventories but different planning conditions: causal-only, causal-plus-intentional, and intentional with permitted ambiguity. Compare consistency and reader interpretations of character motives.

[CALVINO-017] The unity of Invisible Cities may be a recurring referent, not a recoverable geography.
QUESTION: When descriptions suggest one imagined place, what entitles us to infer a common physical world rather than a common symbolic, autobiographical, or thematic referent?
MISSING: A representation that preserves conflicting spatial hypotheses while maintaining a usable narrative world.
TEST: Present multiple fictional images and descriptions suggesting a common city. Construct three interpretations: one consistent map, several related locations, and a branching family of incompatible versions. Compare which interpretation better preserves the source material and supports subsequent storytelling.

[CALVINO-018] Calvino suspected that combinatorial order might be a defense against the very uncertainty literature should confront.
QUESTION: Does anticombinatorics enlarge creative possibility, or does it produce a controllable domain by excluding forms of uncertainty that resist representation?
MISSING: A practical criterion for distinguishing productive formal restriction from premature closure.
TEST: Give an author and a generative system an unresolved narrative situation. Compare workflows that require early formal specification with workflows that permit competing interpretations to remain open. Record when ambiguity is resolved and whether useful alternatives disappear.

[CALVINO-005.01] Calvino excluded changing personalities, not all changing constraints.
QUESTION: What distinguishes updating a character's physical availability from updating the dispositions that determine which actions the character might perform?
MISSING: Criteria for distinguishing dispositions from temporary intentions, beliefs, relationships, and physical capacities.

A representation of which experiences produce which changes, including changes that cannot be inferred from observable actions alone.
TEST: Implement the original four-character, twelve-action system under three conditions:

A. Static compatibility rules.

B. Physical state changes only.

C. Physical and dispositional state changes.

Use identical starting characters and action inventories.

Identify which sequences become valid or invalid, and which kinds of character development remain impossible to represent.

[CALVINO-005.02] The narrative requires identifiable secrets that its original combinatorial vocabulary does not represent.
QUESTION: What additional entities must be introduced before Calvino's supposedly simple compatibility rules can be implemented?
MISSING: A specification of secret identity, ownership, knowledge, disclosure, and temporal persistence.

Evidence about whether Braffort's implemented matrices represented these semantic objects or only approximated their effects.
TEST: Construct two distinct twelve-action sequences that have identical actor-action-patient assignments but differ in whether a later acquisition concerns the same secret.

Try to classify both using only the original event vocabulary.

Introduce explicit secret identifiers and repeat.

The resulting difference establishes the minimum additional representational requirement.

[CALVINO-005.03] An unknown condition is not a false condition, and narrative action depends on who knows the difference.
QUESTION: Should narrative anticombinatorics eliminate impossible events, or eliminate events that particular characters cannot knowingly undertake from their situated perspectives?
MISSING: A mechanism for modeling actions undertaken with unjustified confidence, mistaken beliefs, incomplete knowledge, or deliberately misleading information.

A criterion for deciding when an agent should act despite uncertainty.
TEST: Create a shared narrative world containing one concealed fact and two characters.

Run three conditions:

A. The fact is known to both characters.

B. The fact is unknown to one character.

C. One character holds a false belief about the fact.

Keep physical conditions identical.

Compare generated plans, executed actions, failed actions, and belief revisions.

Determine whether a single global compatibility matrix can reproduce the differences.

[CALVINO-005.04] Contradictory desires can belong to one coherent character.
QUESTION: When should contradictory character dispositions be preserved rather than eliminated by an anticombinatorial filter?
MISSING: A general mechanism for representing ambivalence without forcing an immediate resolution into a single intention.

An account of when contradictory behavior should count as character complexity, deception, transformation, or computational error.
TEST: Define a character with two conflicting desires.

Compare:

A. A planner that rejects contradictory desires.

B. HEADSPACEX-style desire representation with single-minded commitments.

C. A model permitting explicit conflict and revision among commitments.

Hold narrative events constant.

Measure differences in available character trajectories, failed plans, and explanations of behavior.

Ask readers whether the resulting actions appear contradictory, complex, or unmotivated.

[CALVINO-005.05] New evidence can restore fictional worlds that previous constraints excluded.
QUESTION: Can an anticombinatorial system remain useful if its candidate worlds are permitted to re-enter consideration after previously being excluded?
MISSING: A formal procedure for updating plausible-world rankings from audience behavior.

A reliable distinction between revising an assumption and contradicting an established observation.

A test for whether an unexpected addition enriches the fictional world or merely repairs a poorly constructed plot.
TEST: Provide three descriptions of a fictional location.

Initially, all three suggest an ordinary terrestrial city.

Introduce a fourth description establishing an impossible or supernatural spatial condition.

Compare:

A. A system that eliminates inconsistent candidate worlds.

B. A system that revises genre and plausibility assumptions.

C. A system that preserves multiple competing interpretations.

Record which earlier observations each system must contradict, reinterpret, or preserve.

[CALVINO-005.06] The programmer's aesthetic constraints are not the characters' subjective constraints.
QUESTION: How can an author-directed generative system evaluate a developing narrative without reducing character autonomy to compliance with a predetermined plot?
MISSING: A representation of aesthetic criteria that permits them to change during composition.

A method for distinguishing deliberate aesthetic exception from inconsistency or opportunistic constraint relaxation.

An account of which aesthetic decisions should belong to the author, the generator, the reader, or their interaction.
TEST: Construct a small narrative generator with independent physical, character, and aesthetic evaluators.

Define an event that:

A. Satisfies physical constraints.

B. Is plausible for a character.

C. Undermines the desired dramatic trajectory.

Compare three systems:

1. Reject the event through an aesthetic filter.

2. Accept the event and revise subsequent narrative goals.

3. Modify the character model to favor the originally desired trajectory.

Determine which interventions preserve consistency, which impose authorial control, and which permit unanticipated narrative development.
