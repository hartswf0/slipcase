ID: CALVINO-005.01

TITLE:
Calvino excluded changing personalities, not all changing constraints.

SOURCE:
Italo Calvino — Working synopsis for L'ordre dans le crime — reproduced by Paul Braffort in "L'ordre dans le crime: Une expérience cybernétique avec Italo Calvino" — Europe, no. 815 (1997), section 2.

PASSAGE:
[QUOTE]
"Dans chaque permutation, après qu’une action de meurtre a eu lieu, la victime ne peut plus accomplir ni subir aucune action."

[QUOTE]
"Chaque personnage pourrait changer dans le déroulement de l’histoire [...] : perdre certaines incompatibilités pu en acquérir d’autres!!!!!!!!"

[QUOTE]
"Pour le moment on renonce à explorer ce domaine."

RESEARCH OBJECT:
A previously obscured distinction between event-dependent constraints on physical states and event-dependent transformations of character dispositions.

LOCAL MOVE:
Calvino already requires an action's occurrence to change what subsequent actions are permissible. Murder changes the availability of a character for subsequent events.

What he postpones is a different problem: actions that transform the character's psychological or interpersonal compatibility conditions.

SOURCE TERMS:
"Ordre des séquences";
"contraintes objectives";
"contraintes subjectives";
"incompatibilités";
"déroulement de l’histoire."

WHAT BECAME STRANGE:
The parent zettel describes a transition from fixed constraints to evolving constraints. But Calvino's original procedure already depends on evolving physical conditions.

The actual omission is narrower and more interesting: the system can represent a character becoming dead but not becoming different.

QUESTION:
What distinguishes updating a character's physical availability from updating the dispositions that determine which actions the character might perform?

DEEPER QUESTION:
Why do discrete physical transformations fit the combinatorial specification more readily than transformations of character, motive, or social relationship?

MECHANISM:
An event changes the conditions against which later events are evaluated.

Physical constraint:
A murdered character cannot subsequently act.

Dispositional constraint:
A previously incompatible action could become compatible following an intervening experience.

The first is included in Calvino's specification. The second is explicitly deferred.

FORMAL SHIFT:
Action sequence
→ current physical conditions
→ [event-dependent exclusion]
→ permissible subsequent actions.

Character experience
→ changed dispositions
→ [recompute subjective compatibility]
→ changed narrative possibilities.

SOURCE FORMALISM:
Twelve named actions distributed among four characters.

Sequential compatibility constraints.

After a murder, its victim cannot participate in subsequent actions.

Calvino explicitly identifies, but does not implement, the acquisition and loss of subjective incompatibilities.

OUR FORMALIZATION:
[OUR FORMALIZATION — NOT SOURCE SYNTAX]

Separate:

P(t) = physical state
D(t) = character dispositions

P(t+1) = PhysicalEffects(action(t), P(t))

D(t+1) = DispositionalEffects(action(t), D(t), P(t))

Admissible(action, t)
  = PhysicalCompatibility(action, P(t))
    AND
    CharacterCompatibility(action, D(t))

Calvino specifies operations resembling the first update, but leaves the second unresolved.

TENSION:
A psychological disposition might change because of an event, without that change being reducible to the event's physical effects.

Conversely, some apparently psychological constraints can be represented through ordinary state variables.

MISSING:
Criteria for distinguishing dispositions from temporary intentions, beliefs, relationships, and physical capacities.

A representation of which experiences produce which changes, including changes that cannot be inferred from observable actions alone.

BOUNDARY:
The synopsis demonstrates that Calvino identified dynamic character incompatibilities. It does not establish that every psychological variable was intended to be computationally modeled.

→ distinction between physical and subjective constraints
→ Brenner, "Creating Dynamic Story Plots with Continual Multiagent Planning" (2010)
→ Young, "Sketching a Generative Model of Intention Management" (2017).

Implement the original four-character, twelve-action system under three conditions:

A. Static compatibility rules.

B. Physical state changes only.

C. Physical and dispositional state changes.

Use identical starting characters and action inventories.

Identify which sequences become valid or invalid, and which kinds of character development remain impossible to represent.

TITLE:
The narrative requires identifiable secrets that its original combinatorial vocabulary does not represent.

SOURCE:

A character who has obtained another's secret need not obtain that same secret again, but may obtain a different secret through another action.

An implicit narrative entity that must be represented before the stated compatibility rule can be executed correctly.

Calvino treats the appropriation of a secret as an action between two characters while simultaneously requiring the computer to distinguish one secret from another.

"réversibilité";

"compatibilité."

The first representation problem is not necessarily how to make characters change.

It is how to represent what their actions are about.

The phrase "a different secret" introduces an entity whose identity is not contained in the action's original binary character relation.

QUESTION:
What additional entities must be introduced before Calvino's supposedly simple compatibility rules can be implemented?

DEEPER QUESTION:
At what point does a finite combinatorial inventory turn into an open-ended ontology of objects, knowledge, obligations, and relationships?

The system must determine whether two actions concern the same information.

Without secret identity, it cannot distinguish obtaining the same secret repeatedly from obtaining several different secrets.

The corresponding problem recurs for distinct wills, motivations, and interpersonal commitments.

FORMAL SHIFT:
Binary character relation
→ action with an additional semantic object
→ [compare object identity and possession]
→ determine whether repetition is redundant or consequential.

SOURCE FORMALISM:
Four characters.

E = (actor, action, patient)

Required richer event:
E = (actor, action, patient, object)

For secret acquisition:

E1 = (A, discovers, B, secret_1)
E2 = (A, extracts, B, secret_2)

secret_1 == secret_2

It cannot be determined from actor, action, and patient alone.

TENSION:
The additional object can make semantic compatibility computable, but every introduced entity creates further questions.

Who possesses the secret?
Who knows it?
Can two characters independently discover it?
Does revealing it change its status?
Can the content of a secret itself change?

Evidence about whether Braffort's implemented matrices represented these semantic objects or only approximated their effects.

BOUNDARY:

ID: CALVINO-005.03

TITLE:
An unknown condition is not a false condition, and narrative action depends on who knows the difference.

SOURCE:
Michael Brenner — "Creating Dynamic Story Plots with Continual Multiagent Planning" — Proceedings of AAAI-10 (2010), pp. 1517–1522, especially pp. 1518–1519.

[PARAPHRASE]
Brenner's Multiagent Planning Language (MAPL) uses multi-valued state variables that can include an unknown value. Characters possess beliefs about the world and may acquire information through sensing, copresence, and communication.

An action depends not only on its physical preconditions but also on what its controlling agent knows. Plans can be revised when observations or communications change an agent's available information.

RESEARCH OBJECT:
Epistemic availability as a distinct condition of narrative action.

LOCAL MOVE:
Brenner separates the physical conditions under which an action is possible from the knowledge conditions under which a character can deliberately execute it.

This makes different characters' beliefs part of the machinery of plot construction.

SOURCE TERMS:
"Multiagent Planning Language";
"multi-valued state variables";
"unknown";
"knowledge preconditions";
"copresence";
"communication";
"temporary subgoals";
"Continual Multiagent Planning."

Calvino asks whether particular actions are compatible.

Brenner's architecture makes that question insufficient.

An action may be physically possible but unavailable to a character because the character does not know how to perform it or does not know that its preconditions hold.

The same physical world can therefore contain different actionable possibility spaces for different characters.

QUESTION:
Should narrative anticombinatorics eliminate impossible events, or eliminate events that particular characters cannot knowingly undertake from their situated perspectives?

DEEPER QUESTION:
Does narrative possibility belong to the world, the character's representation of the world, or the relation between them?

MECHANISM:
A character maintains beliefs about relevant variables.

Perception and communication can update those beliefs.

Changed beliefs can activate goals, invalidate plans, or permit actions previously unavailable to the character.

Continual planning alternates between planning, execution, observation, and revision.

FORMAL SHIFT:

→ agent-specific beliefs and goals
→ [perception, communication, planning]
→ agent-specific action availability
→ revised beliefs and plans.

MAPL extends PDDL.

It uses multi-valued state variables.

It introduces knowledge and commitment preconditions for actions.

It provides sensing, copresence, and communication models.

Brenner presents CMP Agent(S,G), an algorithm that performs monitoring, replanning, action execution, state estimation, and processing of messages or requests.

OUR FORMALIZATION:
[OUR FORMALIZATION — NOT SOURCE SYNTAX]

World state:
W(t)

Belief state of character i:
B_i(t)

Physical executability:
Executable(a, W(t))

Perceived executability:
KnownExecutable(a, B_i(t))

Executable(a, W(t)) = true

while:

KnownExecutable(a, B_i(t)) = false

An observation can change the second condition without changing the first.

TENSION:
Brenner's MAPL places knowledge conditions on autonomous action.

R. Michael Young's later HEADSPACE research explicitly accommodates actions undertaken because characters mistakenly believe their physical preconditions hold.

These approaches do not treat belief-relative executability identically.

MISSING:
A mechanism for modeling actions undertaken with unjustified confidence, mistaken beliefs, incomplete knowledge, or deliberately misleading information.

A criterion for deciding when an agent should act despite uncertainty.

BOUNDARY:
MAPL and CMP provide a concrete planning architecture, not a complete theory of literary interpretation or aesthetics.

Brenner explicitly distinguishes the capacity to generate dynamic plots from the capacity to generate interesting ones.

TITLE:
Contradictory desires can belong to one coherent character.

SOURCE:
R. Michael Young — "Sketching a Generative Model of Intention Management for Characters In Stories: Adding Intention Management to a Belief-Driven Story Planning Algorithm" — AIIDE Workshop on Intelligent Narrative Technologies (2017), pp. 281–287, especially pp. 282–284.

Young's HEADSPACEX representation permits a character to possess desires supported by inconsistent motivations and even desires for contradictory conditions.

Desires become intentions when a character adopts specific goals and plans.

In the initial implementation, characters revise intentions in response to new beliefs but do not freely adopt goals inconsistent with those to which they are already committed.

A distinction between contradictory desires, contradictory intentions, and contradictory descriptions of the physical world.

LOCAL MOVE:
Young represents desires separately from intentions and allows conflicting dispositions to coexist without immediately treating them as planning inconsistencies.

"intention revision."

A character who wants incompatible outcomes is not necessarily an incoherent character.

When should contradictory character dispositions be preserved rather than eliminated by an anticombinatorial filter?

DEEPER QUESTION:
Can a computational character change through conflict among existing motivations rather than through a rule that directly rewrites personality attributes?

MECHANISM:
A character possesses multiple desires.

Contextual conditions influence which desires become intentions.

Adopted intentions organize plans.

New information can invalidate those plans and trigger revision or abandonment.

The resulting behavior depends on both the character's dispositions and its commitments.

FORMAL SHIFT:
Potentially conflicting desires
→ contextual motivations
→ [intention adoption]
→ committed plan
→ [new beliefs or failure]
→ revised intention state.

SOURCE FORMALISM:
HEADSPACEX defines:

Belief states containing beliefs held true, held false, or unknown.

Desires as tuples of character, motivation conditions, and a desired epistemic literal.

Intentions as tuples including character, adoption state, motivations, goal condition, and plan.

Plans represented through partial-order causal-link structures.

The paper explicitly allows inconsistent desires while limiting intention adoption through single-minded commitment.

OUR FORMALIZATION:
[OUR FORMALIZATION — NOT SOURCE SYNTAX]

Character c desires:

D1: protect another character
D2: expose that character's secret

Both desires may remain active.

Context K1 may activate D1 as an intention.
Context K2 may activate D2.

The resulting tension is not:

WorldState = logically inconsistent

but:

CharacterDesires = potentially incompatible

An intention-selection mechanism must decide which commitments become actionable.

A strict narrative planner can maintain consistent commitments by excluding contradictory intentions.

A literary representation may instead need ambivalence, indecision, self-deception, and action inconsistent with previously stated commitments.

Young's initial HEADSPACEX model uses single-minded commitment, while acknowledging the need for a wider range of character-specific commitment policies.

MISSING:
A general mechanism for representing ambivalence without forcing an immediate resolution into a single intention.

An account of when contradictory behavior should count as character complexity, deception, transformation, or computational error.

BOUNDARY:
HEADSPACEX does not establish that contradiction automatically produces compelling characters.

Its initial commitment policy should not be mistaken for a comprehensive psychological model.

TITLE:

SOURCE:

Siler distinguishes logically possible worlds from worlds an audience considers plausible.

In classical possible-worlds reasoning, new observations eliminate worlds inconsistent with the evidence.

In the proposed alternative, plausibility judgments are nonmonotonic. An audience can encounter evidence that forces it to reconsider worlds previously excluded by default assumptions or genre conventions.

"plausible worlds";

"implicit questions";

The paper presents a research proposal rather than a demonstrated complete implementation.

TENSION:

A reliable distinction between revising an assumption and contradicting an established observation.

BOUNDARY:

Nonmonotonic revision does not license changing facts already established as immutable commitments.
