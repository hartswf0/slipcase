Introduction: a secret that is not in the data

A computer is instructed to reconstruct a story from four people and twelve named actions. Each action must be assigned a pair of distinct characters. The elementary representation looks manageable: twelve actions, twelve available ordered pairs of distinct characters, and therefore 1212 possible assignments before any restrictions are introduced. Yet the moment the machine is told that one character cannot obtain the same secret twice, its seemingly complete vocabulary fails. The action has an actor and a patient; it has no field in which one secret can be distinguished from another. The next problem is not how to search faster. It is what kind of thing a secret must become for a search to run at all.

This unnoticed representational demand is a productive way to return to Italo Calvino’s The Burning of the Abominable House and his collaboration with Paul Braffort. The familiar story of the experiment emphasizes the vastness of the possibility space and the need for constraints to eliminate impossible combinations. Calvino, on this reading, anticipates generative AI by staging the progressive selection of a particular work from innumerable alternatives. The computer would handle routine combinations and leave the artist the consequential clinamen: the departure from a rule that turns an admissible sequence into literature (Braffort 1997; Duncan 2012).

That comparison becomes misleading precisely where it seems easiest. A combinatorial solver and a probabilistic language model do not perform the same operation. The first needs an inventory of candidate assignments and explicit compatibility procedures; the second learns conditional distributions and generates sequences without enumerating all complete stories. A diffusion model likewise learns reverse transitions through noisy representations rather than rejecting entire candidate images (Ho, Jain, and Abbeel 2020). The weak analogy—many possibilities become one artifact—does not explain why some outputs count as coherent worlds, why characters act, or why a reader would care.

The stronger historical proposition is that anticombinatorics exposes a problem of representational commitments. Every attempted reduction of possibility depends on prior decisions about the entities that exist, the state that persists, the observer whose knowledge counts, and the assumptions that can be revised. The surviving Calvino–Braffort material is unusually revealing because these decisions come apart. It handles physical consequences, gestures toward psychological transformation, requires semantic objects its first event schema does not specify, and finally distinguishes the programmer’s aesthetic constraints from the constraints on fictional characters. The projected novel did not appear, but these mismatches are evidence about a research problem, not merely about inadequate processing power (Braffort 1997).

This article follows that problem into an alternative history of computational storytelling and returns to contemporary generative AI. It argues for a distinction between the consistency of an imagined world and the continuing freedom to revise one’s hypothesis about that world. The distinction matters especially when several images or episodes appear to suggest a single place. Such evidence can warrant a shared fictional world, several related worlds, or an irreducibly ambiguous one. An algorithm that settles the issue in advance may destroy the very relation the images were meant to make possible.

I. The number and what it cannot count

Braffort’s retrospective account separates objects often collapsed into one: Calvino’s published fiction, the synopsis given to Braffort, the selection programs actually written, the 1977 presentation, and the unfinished ambition of L’ordre dans le crime. The paper presented in 1977 was assembled from Calvino’s own French translation of the story and the working synopsis. Braffort reports that the experimental programs introduced logical and semantic compatibility matrices but did not tame the combinatorial explosion. A completed novel was not the output of this machine (Braffort 1997). Distinguishing narrative premise, specification, implementation, demonstration, and projection prevents imagined capabilities from becoming historical achievements.

The elementary arithmetic makes the distinction tangible. Four named people yield 4 × 3 = 12 ordered nonreflexive actor–patient pairs. Assign one such pair independently to each of twelve named actions, and the number of assignments is 1212 = 8, 916, 100, 448, 256. The number printed in Calvino’s story is different; Alessandrini identifies the arithmetic discrepancy (Alessandrini 2023). The error does not undermine the elementary combinatorics, but the correct number does not count literary plots either. Two assignments may describe equivalent narratives; one assignment may demand contradictory temporal states; another may be formally permissible yet dramatically inert. Counting assignments is not counting stories.

The synopsis already tries to close that gap. It limits repeated acts and their reversals and demands that certain acts exclude later possibilities. Most plainly, once a murder occurs, the victim can no longer act or suffer another action in the sequence (Braffort 1997). This is an event-dependent rule: whether an action is admissible depends on what has previously happened. It is consequently inaccurate to say that Calvino’s original method knew only static constraints. It knew at least one kind of changing state, the physical availability of a character.

What the synopsis postpones is more precise. Characters might lose or acquire subjective incompatibilities after actions they perform or suffer. Calvino notes the possibility and explicitly declines, for the moment, to explore it (Braffort 1997). The gap is not change versus stasis but one kind of change versus another. A murder updates a discrete condition with a comparatively stable interpretation. Betrayal, seduction, grief, or revelation may change what a character expects, intends, fears, or can imagine doing, without offering an equally simple transition rule. Nor need those changes coincide with outward acts. A person might discover a secret without acting on it, or act on a false assumption and never learn that it was false.

The action schema reveals still another omission. In the rules for acquiring secrets, it matters whether the same secret has been obtained again or whether a different secret is involved. But an event represented only as (actor, action, patient) cannot express that distinction. It needs at least an object identity and some state of possession or knowledge. Once that object is added, further questions arrive: whether a secret can be independently learned, falsely believed, disclosed to several people, or cease to be secret. These are not ornamental refinements. They determine which sequences the machine can correctly exclude. Anticombinatorics first requires the invention of things whose differences have to be judged.

II. The missing state is not only physical

A parallel computational history clarifies what this invention entails. In 1977, the year of Calvino’s Centre Pompidou presentation, James Meehan published TALE-SPIN. Instead of filtering a complete inventory of crime assignments, the system generated stories through a problem solver, an assertion mechanism, and an inference mechanism. Agents pursue goals; their actions update descriptions of the physical and interpersonal world; inferred consequences may produce new goals (Meehan 1977). TALE-SPIN was not a completed answer to literary creation. Its importance here is architectural. Narrative possibility was organized as a sequence of world-state changes, not merely as a set of survivors after elimination.

The chronology does not establish direct influence between Meehan and Calvino or Braffort. It disproves a simpler genealogy in which explicit combinatorics in the 1970s gives way only much later to the simulation of dynamic worlds. A competing representational strategy existed at the time. The distinction between the strategies is causal, not merely historical. In a compatibility filter, a candidate combination may be ruled out by a predicate. In a world simulator, events produce the very state against which later predicates are evaluated. Filtering and simulation can be combined, but they make different choices about what must be given in advance (Meehan 1977; Braffort 1997).

In 1981 Natalie Dehn pressed a further objection. A writer does not always establish a world and allow its inhabitants to generate a plot. The writer may first want a confrontation, revelation, or reversal and then invent a place, relationship, or prior event that makes the desired scene possible. Author-directed generation therefore works backward as well as forward: the narrative goal can induce the construction of conditions under which it can occur (Dehn 1981). This possibility defeats a common assumption shared by naive reconstruction and naive simulation—that there must be one preexisting world whose hidden details the process is recovering. In fictional creation, an apparently missing cause may not be waiting to be found. It may have to be invented.

Later planning research formalizes some of the omitted machinery. Riedl and Young’s IPOCL planner distinguishes causal coherence from character intentionality. Actions must belong to causally intelligible plans and to an explanation of why particular characters perform them (Riedl and Young 2010). Brenner’s continual multiagent planning extends the state of a storyworld to perceptions, communication, commitments, beliefs, goals, and revision. A character may know, suspect, or fail to know a relevant precondition. Two agents can inhabit the same physical environment and face different actionable possibilities because their representations of that environment differ (Brenner 2010). These developments sharpen the problem Calvino deferred: not merely whether events are jointly possible, but for whom, at what time, and under which beliefs.

Young’s later HEADSPACEX proposal adds a particularly useful distinction. A character may possess desires with incompatible ends, even where the system limits the intentions that can be adopted as commitments (Young 2017). Psychological contradiction cannot simply be treated as logical failure. A character who wants to protect another person and also reveal that person’s secret is not necessarily malformed. The coexistence of these desires may be the plot’s central pressure. A system that uses consistency checks indiscriminately may rule out complex characters while faithfully enforcing its own type system.

These advances do not make a simulated story aesthetically adequate. Brenner expressly states that plot quality or interestingness is not an explicit concept in the proposed system and that a model of author or reader judgment would be required to pursue it (Brenner 2010). Better world state, more precise belief state, and richer character agency do not decide which completed sequence counts as a work of art. This is a recurring separation, not a defect that disappears with each new layer of representation.

III. The programmer, the character, and the reader judge different things

Calvino’s synopsis recognizes this separation. Its categories distinguish objective restrictions, subjective constraints derived from character personalities, and aesthetic constraints—also described as subjective constraints of the programmer. It asks whether the latter two can be considered together (Braffort 1997). The question is almost a diagram of the unfinished project. A rule about whether a corpse can act belongs to the state of the fictional world. A rule about whether a character would betray a friend belongs to a model of that character. A rule about whether the betrayal comes too early or creates a dull ending belongs to the maker’s aesthetic evaluation. These rules may overlap in practice, but they do not derive their authority from the same place.

That difference matters for the clinamen. Duncan shows that Calvino’s early account of a literary machine in “Cybernetics and Ghosts” assigns a decisive role to reception, whereas the later anticombinatorial account returns creative privilege to the authorial deviation (Calvino 1986; Duncan 2012). Within Oulipian practice, a stricter formulation of clinamen requires more than an unusual result: a compliant alternative must have been available and deliberately rejected for an aesthetic purpose (Duncan 2012). This condition cannot be met merely by selecting a low-probability token. The sampled token is generally a valid result of the probabilistic sampling process, not evidence that an agent considered a known constraint, found a compliant solution, and chose to violate it.

The strict definition must itself be handled cautiously. Berkman argues that Calvino’s arrangements and revisions do not always instantiate fixed procedures that mechanically generate the final prose. A diagram may organize an evolving work as much as generate it, while the clinamen may be spatial, metaphorical, or present in the ongoing revision of the arrangement (Berkman 2020). The alternative to equating randomness with artistic freedom is therefore not to locate a single, detectable exception in each work. It is to ask what rule was operative for which actor at which stage, how it was changed, and what counterfactual compliant alternative the evidence can actually support.

There is an additional temporal complication in contemporary AI. The human labor of preference ranking, demonstration, filtering, and reward modeling can alter the distribution of future model outputs before any particular writer supplies a prompt (Ouyang et al. 2022). The evaluator may not be the same person as the prompter, reviser, reader, or maker of the training examples. The question of aesthetic agency becomes distributed, but distribution does not make the distinctions disappear. Rewarding instruction-following, preserving semantic consistency, and judging literary consequence are different operations. An optimized distribution can conceal, rather than remove, decisions about which possibilities are considered desirable.

For this reason a simple labor reversal—then the human wrote, now the machine writes—is insufficient. Calvino already considered a machine that might change its own conventions, and later allowed the artist an exceptional role. Contemporary generation likewise encompasses distinct operations: pretraining, preference tuning, decoding, prompting, evaluation, editing, publication, and reception. The significant research task is to locate what each operation treats as fixed and what it can still revise.

IV. The world that evidence does not yet determine

The same problem changes character when the input is not a sequence of events but several images of an imagined place. Conventional reconstruction asks how multiple observations correspond to one spatial scene. Fictional images need not obey that presupposition. They may depict different times, incompatible narrative realities, allegorical versions of a place, or different locations joined by one name. More pixels or more views do not decide which hypothesis ought to be adopted. The question is not only the geometry implied by the image but the ontology and revision policy of the fictional world.

Dehn’s author-directed proposal supplies one alternative to the assumption that a stable world must precede its episodes: the world may be constructed retrospectively to support a desired action (Dehn 1981). Another comes from the different functions of Calvino’s Invisible Cities. Berkman’s account of the novel’s organization and Venice-related interpretive pressure does not require the fifty-five descriptions to resolve into one geometrically coherent Venice (Berkman 2020). A recurrence can be semantic or mnemonic, not merely spatial. Inferring one map from every city description would be an interpretive decision, not a neutral reconstruction of observations.

An especially provocative proposal is Cory Siler’s open-world narrative generation, which distinguishes facts already established to an audience from further worlds it presently finds plausible (Siler 2022). Its key idea is nonmonotonicity: evidence can defeat an assumption that previously made a fictional explanation implausible. A dragon introduced late in the story may not contradict any earlier observation; it may overturn the reader’s default belief that dragons do not exist in this genre or setting. A purely subtractive possibility-space account cannot capture the return of a possibility that was rejected only provisionally. The distinction between an immutable observation and a revisable default is therefore central. This is a proposal rather than a demonstrated general solution, and its source remains a verification priority.

The resulting model is neither indiscriminate openness nor the pursuit of a single final answer. Consider three images that suggest one city but disagree about a tower’s location, the coastline, and a building’s interior. One interpretation treats them as views of a stable city from different times; another treats them as distinct cities with a common emblem; a third treats them as mutually inconsistent but narratively related worlds. Each may preserve important aspects of the images. Before reconstructing an environment, a system should distinguish what was observed, what has been inferred, what has been stipulated for the purposes of the story, and what remains negotiable.

This is also where the analogy with diffusion models should stop. A diffusion model can generate vivid landscapes through learned reverse dynamics (Ho, Jain, and Abbeel 2020). It does not follow from that fact alone that a set of its images describes one causal, persistent, navigable location. A scene may appear photographically resolved while its spatial identity remains underdetermined. Conversely, a world with relatively schematic images can possess a well-specified geography and history. The aesthetic resolution of an image, the computational convergence of a sampling trajectory, and the ontological determination of a world are different kinds of closure.

V. An architecture of qualified commitments

The combined evidence supports a layered model rather than one universal consistency filter. The model is not attributed to Calvino or to any of the later systems; it is an analytical proposal designed to expose decisions usually hidden inside the language of narrative possibility. It separates observations from world hypotheses, world hypotheses from character beliefs, character beliefs from intentions, and all of these from aesthetic criteria. Aesthetic evaluation can propose revisions, but those revisions must reveal whether they change a previously observed fact, a defeasible inference, an agent’s belief, or a desired dramatic trajectory.

Layer What it represents What may change What should not be silently changed
Observed commitments What the story has shown or explicitly stipulated New observations may be added Earlier observations without disclosed revision
World hypotheses Possible causal, spatial, and social states Defaults, inferred details, alternate configurations Their evidential status
Character perspectives What each agent believes, knows, desires, intends Beliefs, motives, goals, commitments Attribution of one character’s belief to another
Aesthetic purposes Desired rhythm, pattern, ambiguity, genre, surprise Preferences and priorities through revision Their ownership and history

The categories are not mutually independent in lived composition. An aesthetic purpose may cause the author to invent a new event; the event may alter what a character knows; that knowledge may activate a plan; the plan may create an unforeseen outcome that changes the author’s initial purpose. The point of separation is not to make these processes autonomous but to make their crossings accountable. What changes, who authorizes the change, and which alternatives remain visible become first-class questions.

The smallest computational test follows from the problem of the secret. Begin with the same twelve action assignments and compare a representation containing only actor, action, and patient with one that additionally identifies secrets and tracks who knows them. Construct two sequences identical at the first level but different in whether a second acquisition concerns the same secret. If the primitive representation cannot distinguish them, no amount of search-space pruning can apply the stipulated rule correctly. The next test gives two characters different beliefs about the same secret. The same world state now supports different available plans. A third test introduces incompatible desires and checks whether the system discards a potentially revealing character conflict. A fourth introduces a new image whose apparent contradiction can be resolved by revising a genre assumption, not an established fact.

These are proposed discriminating experiments, not reported results. Their value is methodological. They locate an error’s level before turning it into a model-quality score. A semantic-object error cannot be repaired by more sampling; an epistemic error cannot be repaired by stricter global consistency; a genre-default error need not be repaired by changing the fictional geography; and an aesthetic disagreement may not be an error at all. The history of computational storytelling suggests that different kinds of failure demand different representational remedies.

Discussion: anticombinatorics after generation

The strongest counterargument is that learned models might eventually absorb all these distinctions statistically. A sufficiently trained generative model can often maintain character identity, obey explicit world constraints, construct plausible causal histories, and revise a narrative in response to instructions. It may not need symbolic representations of secrets, intentions, or observation commitments to produce convincing outputs. This possibility should be taken seriously. Explicit formalisms are not the only means of achieving a behavior, and an architecture’s internal representations need not resemble those used to evaluate it.

Yet there is a difference between achieving an output in one trial and exposing the conditions under which the output remains valid across revisions, characters, and contradictory observations. If the user asks why a once-impossible city can now contain a tower, the system must either articulate the changed commitments or leave the explanation opaque. The demand for an explicit account becomes especially strong in interactive worlds, where characters and users will continue to act and new material will be absorbed. Probabilistic fluency can postpone this accounting. It cannot by itself determine whether the new tower contradicts prior observation, replaces a provisional inference, or deliberately introduces a rupture in the fiction.

Another counterargument is literary. Great literature may violate causal logic, treat location as metaphor, or cultivate characters whose motives never become clear. A perfectly explicit world model might therefore be not only unnecessary but hostile to the work. That challenge is consistent with the argument here. The proposed layers are an instrument for preserving the right kinds of ambiguity, not a demand to resolve everything into a database. A system can explicitly represent that two incompatible versions remain live, or that a narrator’s testimony is disputed, without deciding which is true. The failure is not the absence of a final answer; it is the unacknowledged conversion of an unresolved question into an invisible fixed assumption.

Calvino’s own suspicion of the comfort offered by finite combinatorial order complicates any program of unlimited formalization (Calvino 1986). The aim should not be to inventory every conceivable emotional, social, or aesthetic variable. Instead, representations should emerge from the operations a work requires, remain contestable, and be revised when a new scene makes an earlier distinction inadequate. A model of a city may begin with shared landmarks and later require competing histories. A model of a character may begin with a goal and later require ambivalence. A model of an object may begin as a prop and later become the secret on which the plot turns. The research object is the transformation of the representation itself.

Limitations and unresolved territory

This argument is a close interpretive and technical comparison, not a reconstruction of Braffort’s executable programs. The original source code, exhaustive test outputs, and the complete working dossier were not available in the materials used here. Braffort’s account is primary testimony written later, and claims about implementation must remain narrower than claims about the synopsis (Braffort 1997). The related planning systems were produced in different research communities; resemblance and chronological proximity do not establish direct influence. The discussion of Siler relies on the supplied zettel and a source URL that was blocked during this preparation; its details should be independently rechecked before publication.

The proposed layered model is not a complete computational architecture. It lacks a general mechanism for forming or ranking world hypotheses, a complete treatment of unreliable perception and deliberate deception, and a non-arbitrary account of revising aesthetic criteria. It also leaves unresolved the question of whether preserving contradictory possible worlds improves an actual reader’s experience. The paper therefore proposes discriminating tests rather than claiming evidence of aesthetic superiority.

Conclusion: keep the possibility space answerable

The lasting difficulty in Calvino’s anticombinatorial project was not simply that there were too many stories to search. The rules needed entities the original representation could not identify, characters whose states changed in ways the system deferred, and aesthetic judgments that could not be read off the world’s physical or psychological constraints. Computational storytelling subsequently developed richer representations of goals, beliefs, and revision, but this progress repeatedly disclosed new questions of judgment rather than dissolving them.

Contemporary generative AI changes the production of prose and images without making these questions obsolete. It can generate a compelling outcome without providing an explicit inventory of the possibilities excluded or the assumptions fixed along the way. The decisive design problem becomes how to preserve, contest, and revise those commitments as the fiction continues. An imagined world is not necessarily a latent object waiting for its last missing view. It can be a negotiated construction whose incomplete identity makes further stories possible. In that sense, the most fertile anticombinatorics may not be the operation that narrows a vast field to one survivor. It may be the discipline of knowing when a possibility was never represented, when it was provisionally excluded, and when the work requires letting it return.
