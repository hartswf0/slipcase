===== 001 · instability-by-subtraction · BMC-MESS-001 =====
ZETTEL

ID: BMC-MESS-001

TITLE:
Black Mountain College did not merely suffer instability; it removed several of the devices institutions normally use to suppress it.

SOURCE:
Carl DiSalvo — “Black Mountain College Was a Mess” — 2026 — n.p.

SOURCE URL:
https://underthecircumstances.substack.com/p/black-mountain-college-was-a-mess

PASSAGE:
[QUOTE] “In fact, it seems it was almost intentionally unstable, or at least, many of the things that usually provide institutional stability were purposefully omitted from its operations.”

RESEARCH OBJECT:
Institutional instability produced by subtraction: accreditation, routine, hierarchical authority, fixed curricula, predictable staffing, and conventional evaluation are not merely absent defects but possible stabilizers deliberately weakened or withheld.

LOCAL MOVE:
DiSalvo changes “mess” from a description of organizational failure into a question about what the institution chose not to stabilize.

SOURCE TERMS:
“intentionally unstable”
“institutional stability”
“purposefully omitted”
“authority and routine”
“mess”

WHAT BECAME STRANGE:
Stability stops looking like the neutral baseline of institutional design. It becomes an achievement produced by particular mechanisms that can themselves be refused.

QUESTION:
What institutional mechanisms normally convert an educational experiment into a stable organization, and which of those mechanisms did Black Mountain College deliberately refuse?

DEEPER QUESTION:
Can instability itself be designed, or can institutions only design conditions that repeatedly regenerate instability?

MECHANISM:
[OUR INFERENCE] Remove or weaken stabilizing mechanisms
→ decisions remain locally revisable
→ conflicts cannot be permanently proceduralized
→ participants must repeatedly reproduce the institution through action
→ responsiveness survives at the cost of predictability.

FORMAL SHIFT:
<institutional disorder>
→ <absence or weakening of stabilizing mechanisms>
→ [REPEATED LOCAL RECONSTRUCTION]
→ <persistent but revisable institution>

SOURCE FORMALISM:
NONE

OUR FORMALIZATION:
[OUR FORMALIZATION — NOT SOURCE SYNTAX]

Let S = set of institutional stabilizers.
Let R = capacity for local revision.
Let P = predictability of operations.

BMC appears to operate by selectively reducing S,
accepting lower P in exchange for preserving R.

The unresolved question is whether some minimum S* is necessary for the institution to persist at all.

TENSION:
DiSalvo also emphasizes that faculty and students “worked incredibly hard at maintaining an institution.” Instability therefore cannot simply mean absence of maintenance. Considerable maintenance may have been required precisely to keep the organization from becoming either fully ordered or fully dissolved.

MISSING:
Which specific rules, routines, accounting practices, property arrangements, fundraising practices, interpersonal authorities, or administrative procedures supplied the hidden stability that made visible instability survivable?

BOUNDARY:
The essay does not establish that instability was consciously designed as a unified institutional strategy. DiSalvo explicitly qualifies the claim with “almost intentionally unstable, or at least.”

CITATION TRAIL:
Martin Duberman, Black Mountain: An Exploration in Community.
Faculty meeting minutes and administrative records used by Duberman.
Research on temporary organizations, negotiated order, institutional maintenance, and organizational slack.

TEST:
Inventory the institutional stabilizers present at comparable accredited colleges between 1933 and 1957, then code Black Mountain records for which were eliminated, weakened, informally substituted, or preserved. Determine whether instability resulted from principled refusal, financial incapacity, historical circumstance, or some mixture.

===== 002 · maintain-the-mess · BMC-MESS-002 =====
ZETTEL

ID: BMC-MESS-002

TITLE:
If the mess is the work, maintenance means preventing both collapse and cleanup.

SOURCE:
Carl DiSalvo — “Black Mountain College Was a Mess” — 2026 — n.p.

SOURCE URL:
https://underthecircumstances.substack.com/p/black-mountain-college-was-a-mess

PASSAGE:
[QUOTE] “But what if the mess is the work?”

[QUOTE] “It had to be relatively contained, occasionally rearranged. But it also had to remain a mess.”

RESEARCH OBJECT:
A peculiar maintenance problem: sustaining a productive condition that ordinary organizational maintenance would attempt to eliminate.

LOCAL MOVE:
DiSalvo reverses the normal relationship between work and disorder. The mess is no longer an obstacle surrounding the work; managing without extinguishing it becomes the work.

SOURCE TERMS:
“the mess is the work”
“relatively contained”
“occasionally rearranged”
“remain a mess”
“maintaining”

WHAT BECAME STRANGE:
“Maintenance” usually means restoring a preferred stable state. Here maintenance may mean preventing stabilization itself.

QUESTION:
What kind of maintenance practice keeps a system inside a productive zone of disorder without allowing either bureaucratic closure or organizational collapse?

DEEPER QUESTION:
Is there an institutional equivalent of maintaining a dynamic regime rather than maintaining an equilibrium?

MECHANISM:
[OUR INFERENCE]
disorder exceeds tolerable bounds
→ local intervention / rearrangement
→ immediate failure is prevented
→ intervention stops before eliminating the conditions producing experimentation
→ generative disorder returns.

FORMAL SHIFT:
<mess>
→ <bounded dynamic condition>
→ [CONTAIN / REARRANGE / RELEASE]
→ <continued experimental capacity>

SOURCE FORMALISM:
NONE

OUR FORMALIZATION:
[OUR FORMALIZATION — NOT SOURCE SYNTAX]

Let M(t) = organizational disorder.

There appear to be two failure thresholds:

M(t) < L  → excessive stabilization / loss of experiment
M(t) > U  → institutional breakdown

The maintenance problem becomes:

keep L < M(t) < U

not:

minimize M(t).

TENSION:
The college eventually closed. If closure represents M(t) > U, then the history contains evidence both for the viability and for the limits of this maintenance regime.

MISSING:
What were the actual lower and upper boundaries? Which conflicts were tolerated, and which triggered emergency intervention?

BOUNDARY:
DiSalvo proposes this as an interpretation. The essay does not demonstrate that Black Mountain participants themselves conceptualized their organizational practice as preserving a bounded level of mess.

CITATION TRAIL:
Black Mountain College faculty meeting minutes.
Duberman’s accounts of crises and interventions.
Organizational research on “edge of chaos,” loosely coupled systems, requisite variety, and dynamic stability—but only after checking whether those concepts clarify rather than merely redescribe the case.

TEST:
Construct a chronology of major Black Mountain crises. For each, record the disorder, proposed solution, adopted intervention, and whether the intervention permanently standardized practice or merely restored enough capacity for experimentation to continue.

===== 003 · shadow-transcript · BMC-MESS-003 =====
ZETTEL

ID: BMC-MESS-003

TITLE:
Black Mountain preserved the bureaucratic artifact it publicly declared educationally meaningless.

SOURCE:
Carl DiSalvo — “Black Mountain College Was a Mess” — 2026 — n.p.

SOURCE URL:
https://underthecircumstances.substack.com/p/black-mountain-college-was-a-mess

PASSAGE:
[QUOTE] “At Black Mountain College they did away with grades (of course). But also they also kept a record of grades so, if needed, students could have transcripts.”

RESEARCH OBJECT:
Shadow infrastructure: a rejected institutional representation is secretly or secondarily maintained because outsiders still require it.

LOCAL MOVE:
DiSalvo gives a concrete mechanism by which Black Mountain could refuse a conventional educational value internally without making its students completely illegible externally.

SOURCE TERMS:
“did away with grades”
“kept a record”
“if needed”
“transcripts”

WHAT BECAME STRANGE:
A representation can be institutionally disavowed yet operationally preserved.

QUESTION:
What does it mean to abolish a representation if the institution must continue producing that representation at its boundary?

DEEPER QUESTION:
Can experimental institutions escape dominant standards, or do they survive by running conventional representational systems in shadow form?

MECHANISM:
internal educational practice rejects grades
→ external institution requests conventional transcript
→ latent grade records are retrieved
→ student becomes legible to external bureaucracy
→ internal anti-grade practice survives without requiring the external world to share it.

FORMAL SHIFT:
<learning without grades>
→ <latent grade record>
→ [TRANSLATE ON DEMAND]
→ <externally legible transcript>

SOURCE FORMALISM:
NONE

OUR FORMALIZATION:
[OUR FORMALIZATION — NOT SOURCE SYNTAX]

Internal state I ≠ externally required representation E.

Boundary function:

T(I) → E

The institution can therefore refuse E as its governing internal representation while retaining the ability to emit E when crossing institutional boundaries.

TENSION:
Keeping grades may mean that grades were not actually abolished so much as displaced from the student-facing interface. The distinction between eliminating a metric and hiding a metric becomes consequential.

MISSING:
Who assigned these grades? When? According to what criteria? Were they generated continuously, retrospectively, or only when a transcript was requested?

BOUNDARY:
The essay supplies the existence of retained grade records but not their administrative mechanics.

CITATION TRAIL:
Black Mountain College registrar records and surviving transcripts.
Student accounts of transcript preparation.
Graduate-school correspondence requesting Black Mountain records.
Scholarship on boundary objects, infrastructural inversion, commensuration, and institutional legibility.

TEST:
Compare surviving internal course records with transcripts sent to external institutions. Determine whether grades functioned continuously inside Black Mountain or were translations constructed specifically for boundary crossing.

[[Representations Required to Leave the World That Rejects Them]]

[[Shadow Infrastructure]]

===== 004 · open-book-epistemology · BMC-MESS-004 =====
ZETTEL

ID: BMC-MESS-004

TITLE:
Black Mountain’s examination tested whether a student could manufacture warranted knowledge, not reproduce a bounded curriculum.

SOURCE:
Carl DiSalvo — “Black Mountain College Was a Mess” — 2026 — n.p.

SOURCE URL:
https://underthecircumstances.substack.com/p/black-mountain-college-was-a-mess

PASSAGE:
[QUOTE] “The questions weren’t limited to topics covered in courses; they were questions that tested a student’s ability to engage in investigation and analysis.”

[QUOTE] “How do you know the Philippine Islands exist?”

RESEARCH OBJECT:
An examination whose object is the production and justification of an answer under conditions of open inquiry rather than recall of predefined instructional content.

LOCAL MOVE:
DiSalvo uses the examination system to make Black Mountain’s educational philosophy operational rather than merely rhetorical.

SOURCE TERMS:
“investigation”
“analysis”
“written questions”
“oral examination”
“notes”
“library”
“experience”
“anything they had access to”

WHAT BECAME STRANGE:
Removing resource restrictions does not necessarily weaken an examination. It changes what the examination can measure.

QUESTION:
What is an exam measuring once access to information is no longer treated as the scarce capability?

DEEPER QUESTION:
Does an open-resource examination shift education from possession of answers toward construction of warranted claims?

MECHANISM:
question with no bounded course answer
→ student gathers heterogeneous evidence
→ student constructs an account
→ examiners interrogate the account orally
→ competence is judged through inquiry, synthesis, and defense.

FORMAL SHIFT:
<knowledge to be recalled>
→ <question requiring investigation>
→ [SEARCH + SYNTHESIZE + JUSTIFY + DEFEND]
→ <demonstrated capacity for inquiry>

SOURCE FORMALISM:
Two days of written examination followed by an oral examination of indeterminate duration; students could consult notes, library materials, experience, and other available resources.

OUR FORMALIZATION:
[OUR FORMALIZATION — NOT SOURCE SYNTAX]

Traditional examination:
K_student ≈ match(answer, expected_content)

Black Mountain examination:
K_student ≈ quality(investigation(question),
                    evidence,
                    synthesis,
                    justification,
                    defense)

TENSION:
An unbounded inquiry examination may depend heavily on examiner judgment. Freedom from standardized content potentially increases the power of those deciding whether the inquiry counts as sufficient.

MISSING:
Evaluation criteria. How did examiners decide that a student had “learned enough”? How stable were those judgments across students and faculty?

BOUNDARY:
The example establishes an unusual assessment architecture but does not establish that all Black Mountain examinations operated identically or that the method produced superior learning.

CITATION TRAIL:
Black Mountain College examination questions and student examination papers.
Faculty descriptions of Junior-to-Senior Division advancement.
John Andrew Rice on education.
John Dewey only where documentary evidence establishes a relevant connection.

TEST:
Collect surviving examination prompts, student responses, and examiner records. Code what competencies were explicitly or implicitly rewarded: recall, evidence gathering, synthesis, originality, disciplinary knowledge, argument, self-direction, or oral defense.

[[Examination After Information Scarcity]]

===== 005 · method-without-efficiency · BMC-MESS-005 =====
ZETTEL

ID: BMC-MESS-005

TITLE:
Black Mountain was methodical precisely where it refused efficiency.

SOURCE:
Carl DiSalvo — “Black Mountain College Was a Mess” — 2026 — n.p.

SOURCE URL:
https://underthecircumstances.substack.com/p/black-mountain-college-was-a-mess

PASSAGE:
[QUOTE] “It was methodical, but it wasn’t efficient.”

RESEARCH OBJECT:
Method and efficiency separate. Repeated deliberation, participation, manual work, and local adjustment can constitute disciplined method even when they systematically resist optimization.

LOCAL MOVE:
DiSalvo blocks the easy equation of disorder with carelessness by introducing a crucial distinction: Black Mountain could be organized in its attention without being optimized in its operations.

SOURCE TERMS:
“methodical”
“efficient”
“debated”
“hours”
“over and over again”
“doing the work”

WHAT BECAME STRANGE:
Efficiency is not the opposite of mess. An institution may be inefficient because its method deliberately preserves activities that optimization would remove.

QUESTION:
What valuable institutional operations become visible only after method is separated from efficiency?

DEEPER QUESTION:
When does eliminating redundancy, debate, waiting, manual labor, or duplicated representation destroy the mechanism through which an institution learns?

MECHANISM:
recurrent practical problem
→ extended participation and debate
→ temporary decision
→ participants enact decision
→ consequences re-enter future deliberation
→ method persists through repetition rather than optimization.

FORMAL SHIFT:
<organizational inefficiency>
→ <repeated situated procedure>
→ [DELIBERATE / ACT / REVISIT]
→ <institutional learning without procedural closure>

SOURCE FORMALISM:
NONE

OUR FORMALIZATION:
[OUR FORMALIZATION — NOT SOURCE SYNTAX]

Efficiency asks:

minimize resources/time per settled decision.

The proposed Black Mountain method may instead optimize:

maximize opportunities for participation,
revision,
learning,
and continued contestability.

These objective functions are not equivalent.

TENSION:
Inefficiency can also conceal domination, exhaustion, wasted labor, or inability to settle recurring problems. Calling inefficiency “method” risks romanticizing costs borne unequally by participants.

MISSING:
Who paid the cost of the methodical inefficiency? Which faculty, students, workers, administrators, or community members performed the repetitive labor required to keep the experiment open?

BOUNDARY:
The essay establishes neither that all inefficiency was productive nor that inefficiency was intentionally valued in itself.

CITATION TRAIL:
Faculty meeting minutes recording repeated debates.
Labor records surrounding the farm program.
Accounts from students and staff whose labor sustained daily operations.
Organizational scholarship distinguishing redundancy, slack, deliberation, and inefficiency.

TEST:
Select issues repeatedly debated across multiple years. Track whether repetition produced new information, changed positions, redistributed authority, or merely reproduced unresolved conflict. Distinguish generative recurrence from simple institutional failure.

[[Method Without Optimization]]

===== 006 · belief-as-infrastructure · BMC-MESS-006 =====
ZETTEL

ID: BMC-MESS-006

TITLE:
Shared belief may function as infrastructure when rules cannot carry the institution.

SOURCE:
Carl DiSalvo — “Black Mountain College Was a Mess” — 2026 — n.p.

SOURCE URL:
https://underthecircumstances.substack.com/p/black-mountain-college-was-a-mess

PASSAGE:
[QUOTE] “It worked because they had a mess they believed in and were committed to.”

[QUOTE] “What seemed to make it work, for lack of a better word, was faith: a confidence and devotion to the shared endeavor.”

RESEARCH OBJECT:
Commitment as a possible substitute for formal institutional reliability: participants repeatedly supply effort because the collective project is worth reproducing despite unstable rewards, procedures, and outcomes.

LOCAL MOVE:
After rejecting faculty excellence, democracy, and institutional freedom as sufficient explanations, DiSalvo proposes “faith” as the residual mechanism holding the experiment together.

SOURCE TERMS:
“belief”
“commitment”
“faith”
“confidence”
“devotion”
“shared endeavor”

WHAT BECAME STRANGE:
Belief is usually treated as culture surrounding an organization. Here it may be doing operational work normally delegated to contracts, routines, incentives, hierarchy, or predictability.

QUESTION:
Under what conditions can shared belief perform the coordinating work that formal institutional structures normally perform?

DEEPER QUESTION:
Does an experimental institution require unusually high commitment because it externalizes onto participants the coordination labor that bureaucracy normally absorbs?

MECHANISM:
formal guarantees remain weak
→ participants encounter recurring uncertainty and friction
→ shared commitment supplies reasons to continue acting despite those costs
→ continued action reproduces the institution
→ the institution persists without resolving the underlying instability.

FORMAL SHIFT:
<shared educational ideal>
→ <participant commitment>
→ [ABSORB COORDINATION COST]
→ <continued institutional reproduction>

SOURCE FORMALISM:
NONE

OUR FORMALIZATION:
[OUR FORMALIZATION — NOT SOURCE SYNTAX]

Institutional persistence requires recurring coordination cost C.

Ordinary institution:
formal structure F absorbs much of C.

Experimental institution with weak F:
participants must supply discretionary commitment D.

Persistence condition might therefore resemble:

F + D ≥ C.

As F decreases, required D rises.

TENSION:
Dependence on devotion may make an institution inspirational while also making it exploitative. “Faith” can redistribute structural costs into unpaid effort, personal sacrifice, charisma, loyalty, or burnout.

MISSING:
Distribution. Who was required to believe most? Who could afford to remain committed? Who left, and did departures correlate with the amount of uncompensated institutional labor expected of them?

BOUNDARY:
“Faith” is DiSalvo’s retrospective explanatory term. The essay does not yet demonstrate that commitment was either necessary or sufficient for Black Mountain’s persistence.

CITATION TRAIL:
Duberman’s interviews with participants who stayed and participants who left.
Faculty contracts, salaries, tuition arrangements, work obligations, fundraising records.
Albert O. Hirschman on exit, voice, and loyalty.
Research on institutional work, organizational commitment, communes, intentional communities, and high-commitment organizations.

TEST:
Compare testimony from long-term participants, short-term participants, and people who left after conflicts. Ask whether persistence is better predicted by ideological commitment, material dependency, charismatic attachment, social ties, lack of alternatives, or concrete institutional arrangements.

ZETTEL

ID: BMC-MESS-007

TITLE:
Democratic procedure can preserve conservative outcomes inside a radically experimental institution.

SOURCE:
Carl DiSalvo — “Black Mountain College Was a Mess” — 2026 — n.p.

SOURCE URL:
https://underthecircumstances.substack.com/p/black-mountain-college-was-a-mess

PASSAGE:
[QUOTE] “The democratic governance of the school, for which it was renowned, was tenuous and at times served to reinforce conservative positions.”

RESEARCH OBJECT:
Democracy and experimentalism do not necessarily covary. A radical institution can use participatory governance mechanisms that stabilize existing positions rather than continually radicalizing them.

LOCAL MOVE:
DiSalvo introduces a counterexample to the assumption that Black Mountain’s democratic governance straightforwardly explains its experimental character.

SOURCE TERMS:
“democratic governance”
“tenuous”
“conservative positions”
“charisma”
“authority”

WHAT BECAME STRANGE:
Democracy is no longer an explanatory synonym for openness, experimentation, or progressiveness.

QUESTION:
Through what mechanisms can democratic governance conserve arrangements inside an institution founded in opposition to authority and routine?

DEEPER QUESTION:
Did Black Mountain remain experimental because of democratic governance, despite democratic governance, or because conflicts between democracy and other forms of authority were never fully resolved?

MECHANISM:
issue enters collective decision process
→ formally distributed participation
→ unequal charisma, expertise, tenure, alliances, or authority shape deliberation
→ democratic procedure legitimates a decision
→ existing arrangements may become harder rather than easier to contest.

FORMAL SHIFT:
<democratic participation>
→ <unequally weighted deliberation>
→ [COLLECTIVE DECISION]
→ <potential stabilization of existing power>

SOURCE FORMALISM:
NONE

OUR FORMALIZATION:
[OUR FORMALIZATION — NOT SOURCE SYNTAX]

Formal vote/voice equality ≠ effective influence equality.

If participant i has formal voice V_i but effective influence:

I_i = f(V_i, charisma_i, status_i, expertise_i, coalition_i),

then democratic procedure can coexist with highly unequal causal power.

TENSION:
The same democratic procedures may also have made continual contestation possible. Their conservative and experimental effects therefore need to be separated empirically rather than assigned in advance.

MISSING:
Concrete decisions in which democratic procedure reinforced a conservative position, and evidence showing which actors influenced those outcomes.

BOUNDARY:
DiSalvo states the pattern but does not provide a detailed case in this essay sufficient to establish its mechanism.

CITATION TRAIL:
Duberman’s accounts of faculty governance disputes.
Black Mountain faculty meeting minutes.
Specific admissions, labor, race, curriculum, property, and personnel disputes.
Research on informal hierarchy within formally horizontal organizations.

TEST:
Reconstruct one consequential decision from meeting minutes and correspondence. Compare formal decision rights with speaking frequency, proposal authorship, coalition structure, eventual outcome, and retrospective participant accounts.

===== 008 · hidden-curriculum-after-subtraction · BMC-MESS-001-A =====
ZETTEL

ID:
BMC-MESS-001-A

TITLE:
Removing formal structure did not create an open field; it allowed the institution’s strongest existing competencies to become the hidden curriculum.

SOURCE:
Nora Caplan-Bricker — “This North Carolina Campus Was Meant to Show Off the Future of Online Education” — The New Republic — 2013 — https://newrepublic.com/article/116014/black-mountain-sole-mooc-campus-online-learners-has-rough-start

PASSAGE:
[PARAPHRASE] Black Mountain SOLE described itself as self-organized and interdisciplinary, but the structures that actually existed disproportionately served entrepreneurs: staff expertise centered on business, PR, and programming; workshops concerned landing pages, mentors, and press releases; and an initially academic support group eventually became another entrepreneurial support group.

RESEARCH OBJECT:
Institutional subtraction does not produce neutrality. When explicit curriculum and strong formal structures are removed, whatever competencies, habits, schedules, vocabularies, and resources are already most available can become the institution’s effective curriculum without ever being declared as such.

LOCAL MOVE:
Caplan-Bricker gives a counterexample to treating instability or self-organization as intrinsically pluralistic. The SOLE formally allowed many directions, but its available infrastructure repeatedly channeled activity toward entrepreneurship.

SOURCE TERMS:
“guiding structures”
“do it yourself learning”
“business incubator”
“entrepreneurial support group”
“techie tide”
“exchange”

WHAT BECAME STRANGE:
The absence of a prescribed curriculum can itself prescribe. What looks like freedom at the level of rules may become strong selection at the level of available support.

QUESTION:
When an experimental institution removes explicit curricular direction, what determines which practices become its de facto curriculum?

DEEPER QUESTION:
Is “self-organization” ever structurally neutral, or does it necessarily amplify the capacities already best represented among founders, staff, resources, and participants?

MECHANISM:
formal curricular authority is weakened
→ participants are told to organize their own learning
→ some practices possess more mentors, expertise, vocabulary, examples, schedules, and visible success
→ those practices reproduce more easily
→ adjacent activities reorganize around them
→ an undeclared curriculum emerges.

FORMAL SHIFT:
<curricular openness>
→ <unequal distribution of enabling resources>
→ [SELF-ORGANIZE]
→ <emergent curricular asymmetry>

SOURCE FORMALISM:
The source identifies concrete enabling structures: staff expertise in business, PR, and programming; workshops on landing pages, mentorship, and press releases; self-led workshops; and an academic group that shifted toward entrepreneurship.

OUR FORMALIZATION:
[OUR FORMALIZATION — NOT SOURCE SYNTAX]

For practice p:

P(p persists) ≠ function(freedom alone)

P(p persists) = f(
  available_expertise,
  mentors,
  tools,
  schedules,
  exemplars,
  status,
  participant_prior_capability
)

Removing central curriculum C does not yield:

C = 0.

It may yield:

C_formal → 0
C_emergent → distribution of existing capability.

TENSION:
The original Black Mountain College also allowed course offerings to depend heavily on the faculty present. The important distinction may therefore not be “structured versus unstructured,” but whether the resulting asymmetry generates encounters across practices or allows one practice to colonize the rest.

MISSING:
A comparative account of why faculty-specific asymmetry at the historical Black Mountain College produced celebrated cross-disciplinary work while staff-specific asymmetry at SOLE appears, in Caplan-Bricker’s account, to have pulled activity toward entrepreneurship.

BOUNDARY:
The article documents SOLE during its first months. It cannot establish that entrepreneurship would necessarily have remained dominant if the institution had survived and developed additional capacities.

CITATION TRAIL:
[[BMC-MESS-001]]
→ investigate the stabilizers and structures omitted from an experimental institution
→ Caplan-Bricker’s SOLE case
→ omission itself redistributes institutional selection pressure toward whatever structures remain.

[[BMC-MESS-005]]
→ ask what practices survive when efficiency and formal coordination are rejected
→ emergent entrepreneurial curriculum.

TEST:
Create parallel inventories for historical Black Mountain College and Black Mountain SOLE containing faculty/staff expertise, available facilities, scheduled activities, funding sources, participant-initiated activities, and recurring outputs. Test whether supposedly open educational systems converge toward whichever practice has the densest support infrastructure.

===== 009 · freedom-or-drift · BMC-MESS-002-A =====
ZETTEL

ID:
BMC-MESS-002-A

TITLE:
A productive mess may require participants who already possess the capacity to navigate it.

SOURCE:

QUESTION:

<structured versus structureless>

[OUR FORMALIZATION — NOT SOURCE SYNTAX]

Let F = organizational form and T = task.

Adequacy is relational:

A = fit(F, T)

A form may satisfy:

fit(F, T1) > threshold

while later:

fit(F, T2) < threshold.

The research variable is therefore not STRUCTURE_AMOUNT but STRUCTURE/TASK FIT plus the group’s capacity to revise F when T changes.

PASSAGE:
[OUR INFERENCE] Across the cases, openness is repeatedly produced not by eliminating form but by relocating specification into themes, boundaries, resource ratios, facilitation roles, mobility rules, translation interfaces, access infrastructure, or task-specific coordination.

RESEARCH OBJECT:
Structured openness as a discriminating hypothesis: substantive trajectories remain underdetermined while selected conditions are deliberately fixed, maintained, and—if the arrangement is genuinely open—made revisable by some intelligible process.

LOCAL MOVE:
A previously unresolved graph address becomes a provisional card only after additional source tests. Freeman adds task/structure fit; Granny Cloud adds embodied infrastructure. Together they make “structured openness” more specific than the claim that every open system secretly has some structure.

WHAT BECAME STRANGE:
The variables that look most incidental to an ideology of openness—device ratios, meeting duration, centre access, facilitator timing, transcript translation, volunteer availability—can be the variables doing the most causal work.

QUESTION:
Can structured openness discriminate among experimental institutions, or does it collapse into the trivial statement that all organized activity has conditions?

DEEPER QUESTION:
Which conditions must be visible and contestable for openness to remain a capacity for collective revision rather than a rhetoric masking infrastructure or informal power?

MECHANISM:
task T is defined loosely enough to permit discovery
→ selected conditions C are fixed or maintained
→ participants generate trajectories X within those conditions
→ outcomes expose failures or new tasks
→ a constitutional process A either permits revision of C or blocks it
→ the system remains experimentally open only if learning can reach the layer that structures the experiment.

FORMAL SHIFT:
<more structure versus less structure>
→ <placement + task-fit + revisability of constraint>
→ [HOLD SOME VARIABLES / OPEN OTHERS / REVISE THE HOLDING RULES]
→ <structured openness>

SOURCE FORMALISM:
NONE

OUR FORMALIZATION:
[OUR FORMALIZATION — NOT SOURCE SYNTAX]

Represent an experimental arrangement as:

SO = <T, C, X, A>

T = current task or problem field
C = conditions held comparatively fixed
X = participant-generated trajectories left open
A = authority/process by which C may be inspected and revised

A useful structured-openness claim must specify all four.

Failure modes:

1. C invisible      → hidden curriculum / informal power
2. C inaccessible   → participants cannot contest the apparatus
3. T changes, C lags → structural mismatch
4. infrastructure fails → nominal openness becomes unusable
5. X over-specified → openness collapses into ordinary prescription
6. C under-specified → drift / abandonment / domination by prior capacity.

TENSION:
Because every institution has conditions, “structured openness” risks becoming empty unless it predicts differences. The concept earns its place only if the constraint ledger distinguishes cases, failure modes, and interventions better than “structure” or “freedom” alone.

MISSING:
Comparative coding across institutions; archival evidence about Black Mountain College’s actual stabilizers; direct evidence of Open Space transmission into Black Mountain SOLE; observational data about SOLE participation and device control; evidence about who could revise constitutional conditions in each case.

BOUNDARY:
“Structured openness” is [OUR INFERENCE], not terminology used by DiSalvo, Caplan-Bricker, Mitra, Owen, Freeman, or the Granny Cloud. The cited sources supply mechanisms and counterexamples from which the hypothesis is assembled.
