TITLE:
A structureless form can work until the task changes.

SOURCE:
Jo Freeman — “The Tyranny of Structurelessness” — 1972; author-hosted text with archival publication metadata from Duke Digital Repository.

SOURCE URL:
https://www.jofreeman.com/joreen/tyranny.htm
https://repository.duke.edu/dc/wlmpc/wlmms01018

PASSAGE:
[QUOTE] “there is no such thing as a structureless group.”

[PARAPHRASE] Freeman distinguishes loose groups that worked for consciousness-raising from groups trying to undertake more specific coordinated action; problems appeared when the task changed but the organizational form did not change with it.

RESEARCH OBJECT:
Task-relative structure. The relevant question is not whether a group has structure, but whether the structure through which it coordinates is fitted to the work it is now attempting.

LOCAL MOVE:
The earlier SOLE zettels treated horizontality and hidden hierarchy as a topology of power. Freeman adds a temporal operator: a form that is adequate for one task can become disabling when the group’s task changes.

SOURCE TERMS:
“structurelessness”
“formal and informal structures”
“task oriented”
“decision-making”
“responsible”
“delegation”

WHAT BECAME STRANGE:
An organizational form can be successful evidence for itself right up to the moment its task changes. The failure may not be “too little structure” in general but structural lag: yesterday’s adequate form governing today’s different work.

QUESTION:
Which experimental institutions mistake organizational forms optimized for exploration, discussion, or local experimentation for forms adequate to coordination, resource allocation, persistence, or collective action?

DEEPER QUESTION:
Can we diagnose institutional failure by measuring mismatch between current task and inherited coordination structure rather than by measuring an abstract amount of hierarchy?

MECHANISM:
form F supports task T1
→ success stabilizes belief in F
→ group shifts toward task T2
→ F is retained because it signifies openness or identity
→ informal structure carries coordination that explicit structure no longer handles
→ insiders gain legibility and outsiders encounter confusion
→ group founders, informal power hardens, or a new structure is made explicit.

FORMAL SHIFT:
<structured versus structureless>
→ <task × coordination-form fit>
→ [TASK CHANGES / FORM LAGS]
→ <hidden hierarchy OR organizational failure OR explicit redesign>

SOURCE FORMALISM:
Freeman explicitly distinguishes formal and informal structures and gives principles for democratic structuring, including delegated authority, accountability, distributed authority, task rotation, and rational allocation of tasks.

OUR FORMALIZATION:
[OUR FORMALIZATION — NOT SOURCE SYNTAX]

Let F = organizational form and T = task.

Adequacy is relational:

A = fit(F, T)

A form may satisfy:

fit(F, T1) > threshold

while later:

fit(F, T2) < threshold.

The research variable is therefore not STRUCTURE_AMOUNT but STRUCTURE/TASK FIT plus the group’s capacity to revise F when T changes.

TENSION:
Formalization can itself over-constrain a group, preserve obsolete authority, or convert flexible relations into rigid offices. Freeman’s argument does not imply that more formal structure is always better.

MISSING:
Direct evidence that Black Mountain SOLE, Open Space practitioners, or Black Mountain College drew on Freeman. The current use is comparative and conceptual, not genealogical.

BOUNDARY:
Freeman wrote about the women’s liberation movement and political organization. Applying the mechanism to educational experiments requires testing task/structure fit rather than assuming the cases are equivalent.

TEST:
For historical Black Mountain College, Black Mountain SOLE, Mitra-style SOLE, and Open Space, identify the task at each stage, the explicit coordination form, the informal coordination form, and moments when the task changed. Test whether breakdowns cluster where task changes outpace structural revision.
