TITLE:
When accreditation disappeared, evaluation did not disappear; startup metrics rushed in to replace academic ones.

SOURCE:
Nora Caplan-Bricker — “This North Carolina Campus Was Meant to Show Off the Future of Online Education” — The New Republic — 2013 — https://newrepublic.com/article/116014/black-mountain-sole-mooc-campus-online-learners-has-rough-start

PASSAGE:
[PARAPHRASE] SOLE rejected accreditation and contrasted credential value with the value participants themselves generated. Yet elsewhere participants became “case studies,” admissions supplied bodies for “proof of concept,” startups and seed funding became prominent examples of success, and the school struggled to establish a viable business model.

RESEARCH OBJECT:
An institution can abolish one evaluation regime without abolishing evaluation. The vacuum may instead be occupied by another regime whose criteria seem natural because they come from the institution’s surrounding culture rather than from formal educational bureaucracy.

LOCAL MOVE:
Caplan-Bricker complicates the earlier grade/transcript case. Historical Black Mountain preserved conventional academic representation at the boundary; SOLE rejected that representation but appears to have imported startup representations into the interior.

WHAT BECAME STRANGE:
Anti-credentialism can increase rather than decrease measurement if educational worth becomes translated into projects, ventures, traction, demonstrable outcomes, or proof of concept.

QUESTION:
What evaluation regime takes over when an experimental educational institution explicitly refuses grades, degrees, accreditation, and standardized curriculum?

DEEPER QUESTION:
Can an institution refuse dominant representations without importing the representations of another domain?

MECHANISM:
academic credential regime is rejected
→ evaluative vacuum remains
→ institution already possesses startup vocabulary and practices
→ entrepreneurial outputs become visible examples of generated “value”
→ participant and institutional success become legible through startup metrics.

TENSION:
The article does not show that SOLE formally evaluated every participant according to startup metrics. Some participants pursued art, community, bodily practices, or self-exploration. The stronger claim is that entrepreneurial outputs received disproportionate structural support and narrative visibility.

MISSING:
SOLE’s internal documents defining success, progress, participant value, donor reporting, admissions criteria, and “proof of concept.”

BOUNDARY:
The source supports a migration of evaluative vocabulary, not a fully specified replacement assessment system.
