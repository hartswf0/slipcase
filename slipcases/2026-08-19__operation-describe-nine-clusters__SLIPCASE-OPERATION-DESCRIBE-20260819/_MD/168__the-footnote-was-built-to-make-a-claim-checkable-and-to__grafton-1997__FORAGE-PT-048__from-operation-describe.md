TITLE: The footnote was built to make a claim checkable and to assign credit, never to make a process visible — so demanding fidelity of citation misreads the institution

SOURCE: Anthony Grafton, The Footnote: A Curious History (Harvard University Press, 1997) [UNVERIFIED pagination]; read against PROGRAMS/memex.json path integrity and [[FORAGE-PT-021]]

PASSAGE: [PARAPHRASE] Grafton traces the footnote's development as a device by which historians made their assertions verifiable against sources and established authority for their claims — an apparatus of proof and credit whose form was shaped by disputes about reliability, not by any ambition to record how the historian actually worked. [QUOTE] memex.json: "<AI-assisted scholarship> must be judged by <path integrity>, not by <answer fluency>."

RESEARCH OBJECT: A functional mismatch with a documented history. Citation performs two jobs — it lets a reader check a claim against a source, and it attributes priority. Neither requires that the citation record the order in which sources were consulted. The parent found that a trail measures the argument rather than the inference; this child shows the apparatus was designed for the argument on purpose.

LOCAL MOVE: This child historicises the parent's finding. What looked like a defect in citation practice is the practice working as designed, which changes the remedy from "fix citation" to "build a second apparatus".

SOURCE TERMS: footnote / apparatus / proof / authority / verification / credit

WHAT BECAME STRANGE: The two jobs citation does are both *forward-facing* — they let a reader go to the source and check. Process fidelity is *backward-facing* — it tells you how the author got there. Scholarship has never had an apparatus for the second, and has not needed one, because the author's competence was warranted socially rather than evidenced procedurally. The demand for fidelity arrives exactly when that social warrant is withdrawn.

QUESTION: Does any established scholarly apparatus record process rather than warrant — and if lab notebooks and preregistration are the candidates, why did they arise in the sciences and not in the humanities?

DEEPER QUESTION: If fidelity apparatus emerges where competence can no longer be socially warranted, then the demand for reasoning transparency is a *symptom of withdrawn trust* rather than an epistemic advance. That reframes the whole transparency programme as an institutional response to a credibility crisis, and predicts it will be resisted by exactly those whose warrant is still intact.

SOURCE FORMALISM: NONE — historical argument.

OUR FORMALIZATION: [OUR FORMALIZATION — NOT SOURCE SYNTAX] Two apparatus types: FORWARD (citation, bibliography — lets a reader reach the source) and BACKWARD (lab notebook, preregistration, version history — records what the author did and when). Scholarship in the humanities has only the first. The memex demand is for the second, and it is a proposal for a new institution rather than a critique of an old one.

TENSION: READING A: citation was never meant to do this, so the parent's finding is not a scandal and the honest move is to build backward apparatus alongside it. READING B: citation *implies* a process claim in practice — a reader assumes the cited sources informed the argument — so the apparatus makes a promise it was not designed to keep, and the mismatch is a real misrepresentation rather than a category difference.

MISSING: Verified pagination. Any comparative account of why preregistration emerged in some fields and not others. Any survey of what readers actually assume a citation asserts about process.

BOUNDARY: Grafton's history concerns the footnote in historical scholarship. Generalising to all citation practice, and to machine-generated citation, exceeds the source.

TEST: Survey readers on what a citation asserts: that the source supports the claim, that the author read it, that it informed the argument, that it was consulted before the conclusion. The distribution of assumed claims measures whether Reading B's misrepresentation is real in readers' minds.
