ID: FORAGE-PT-021

TITLE: The corpus's most operational criterion is refuted by the corpus's own category-mistake detector

SOURCE: Collision of PROGRAMS/memex.json (path integrity as the criterion) with PROGRAMS/argue.json (logical order is not discovery order)

PASSAGE: [QUOTE] memex.json: "Can a system show not only <what it says>, but <how its path through the record was built>?" [QUOTE] memex.json: "<AI-assisted scholarship> must be judged by <path integrity>, not by <answer fluency>." [QUOTE] argue.json: "prevents the category mistake of reading the finished shape of an argument backward into the living activity of thought."

RESEARCH OBJECT: A direct contradiction between two instruments in one set. The memex test proposes to replace fluency with the trail. The inference detector says any presented sequence is an arrangement, not a history. A trail is a presented sequence. Therefore path integrity replaces one unreliable indicator with another of exactly the same kind — and the corpus contains the refutation of its own remedy.

LOCAL MOVE: Both instruments are trying to defeat the same enemy: a polished artifact taken as evidence of the process behind it. One proposes the trail as the fix; the other, applied to trails, dissolves the fix.

SOURCE TERMS: path integrity / answer fluency / auditable intellectual traversal / finished shape / living activity / logical order / psychological discovery

WHAT BECAME STRANGE: The corpus's eight-way consensus is that fluent output is not evidence of the process. The memex test is the consensus's flagship remedy. But a trail *is* fluent output — output in the genre of citation rather than prose. Making the trail the criterion moves the fluency problem one level up and calls it a solution.

QUESTION: Is there any artifact-inspectable property that distinguishes a recorded path from a reconstructed one, or does the distinction require tamper-evident instrumentation at retrieval time?

DEEPER QUESTION: If it requires instrumentation, then auditability cannot be assessed after the fact — it must be designed in beforehand. Which means the entire apparatus of citation, footnote and bibliography, across every discipline, has never been auditable in the sense the memex test demands. The trail was always an argument.

MECHANISM: <CONCLUSION REACHED BY SOME ROUTE> -> [SOURCES SELECTED THAT SUPPORT IT] -> [ARRANGED IN A DEFENSIBLE ORDER] -> <TRAIL> -> [AUDIT INSPECTS THE TRAIL] -> integrity confirmed -> <FIDELITY NEVER ASSESSED>

FORMAL SHIFT: <FLUENT ANSWER> -> <FLUENT TRAIL> -> [SAME CATEGORY MISTAKE, ONE LEVEL UP] -> <CRITERION FAILS>

SOURCE FORMALISM: NONE — the collision is between two informal criteria.

OUR FORMALIZATION: [OUR FORMALIZATION — NOT SOURCE SYNTAX] Let R_actual be the retrieval sequence and R_reported the cited sequence. INTEGRITY(R_reported) is decidable from the artifact. FIDELITY = agreement(R_actual, R_reported) is not. The memex test as written measures only integrity; its ethical claim requires fidelity; fidelity requires a log the reporter cannot edit.

TENSION: READING A: integrity suffices, because a walkable trail is useful to a reader regardless of how it was assembled — the value is prospective, not historical. READING B: the test's stated purpose is to show *how the path was built*; a reconstructed trail answers a different question and the substitution is exactly the failure the corpus exists to prevent.

MISSING: Any acknowledgement in either file that they collide. meta.json registers five cross-references and not this one — the sharpest contradiction in the set is unregistered.

BOUNDARY: This does not show trails are worthless. It shows the criterion as stated cannot do the work claimed for it.

CITATION TRAIL: Bush, "As We May Think" (1945). Ryle, "Thinking and Inferring" (1953). Attestation and tamper-evident logging. [[FORAGE-PT-002]] [[FORAGE-PT-019]]

TEST: Instrument a retrieval system so that actual retrieval order is logged where the generating process cannot revise it. Compare against reported citations across 50 outputs. The divergence rate is the size of the problem — and the first measurement of whether any trail has ever been a record.
