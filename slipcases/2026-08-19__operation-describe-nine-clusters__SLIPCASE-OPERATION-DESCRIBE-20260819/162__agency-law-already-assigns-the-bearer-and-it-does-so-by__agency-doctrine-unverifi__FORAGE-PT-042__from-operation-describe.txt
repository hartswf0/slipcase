TITLE: Agency law already assigns the bearer, and it does so by requiring the agent to be a person — which is exactly where the gap actually sits

SOURCE: The common-law doctrine of agency — a principal is bound by acts of an agent acting within the scope of authority, and respondeat superior for employer liability [UNVERIFIED for specific jurisdictional formulations]; read against PROGRAMS/dac.json commitment vocabulary

PASSAGE: [PARAPHRASE] Agency doctrine holds that when an agent acts within authority the principal is bound by and answerable for the act; the agent's own standing matters for some purposes but the principal bears the obligation regardless. The doctrine is built for human and corporate agents. [QUOTE] dac.json: "<accountable coordination of commitments, breakdowns, repairs, and responsibilities>"

RESEARCH OBJECT: An existing institutional answer and the precise point where it fails to reach. The parent asked whether a commitment has ever been enforced against a system. Agency law says the question is misdirected: obligations run to the principal, not the instrument. The gap is not that nobody bears the outcome — the deployer does — but that the doctrine's machinery for *scope of authority* presupposes an agent who can understand and exceed instructions.

LOCAL MOVE: This child replaces the parent's search for enforcement against a system with the recognition that the relevant institution never intended to enforce against instruments, and asks instead what the doctrine's presuppositions require.

SOURCE TERMS: principal / agent / scope of authority / respondeat superior / bound / ratification

WHAT BECAME STRANGE: Scope of authority is the load-bearing concept and it is intention-laden. Whether an act falls inside authority depends on what the agent was reasonably given to understand. A system has no understanding to be reasonable about, so every act it takes is either inside authority by construction (the deployer owns everything) or the concept does no work. Either way, the doctrine's discriminating machinery goes idle, and the practical result is unlimited deployer liability or none.

QUESTION: How do existing legal instruments actually delimit authority for automated action — by enumerated permissions, by outcome caps, or not at all?

DEEPER QUESTION: If scope of authority cannot be defined for a non-understanding agent, then deployers face a choice the parent's vocabulary cannot express: accept strict liability for everything the system does, or specify permissions so narrowly that autonomy disappears. That is a design dilemma with a legal shape, and it is the operative form of "carrier, not maker".

MECHANISM: <PRINCIPAL AUTHORISES> -> [AGENT ACTS] -> [COURT ASKS WHETHER ACT WAS WITHIN SCOPE, USING WHAT THE AGENT COULD REASONABLY UNDERSTAND] -> <PRINCIPAL BOUND OR NOT>. With a non-understanding agent the middle test has no content, and the branch collapses.

FORMAL SHIFT: <COMMITMENT> -> <PRINCIPAL-AGENT RELATION> -> [SCOPE-OF-AUTHORITY TEST] -> <TEST WITHOUT CONTENT FOR A NON-UNDERSTANDING AGENT>

SOURCE FORMALISM: The doctrine's tests are stated rules in legal practice; none is quoted here and formulations vary by jurisdiction.

OUR FORMALIZATION: [OUR FORMALIZATION — NOT SOURCE SYNTAX] Three regimes for automated action: STRICT — deployer bears all outcomes, scope undefined; ENUMERATED — authority is an explicit permission list and anything outside is unauthorised; INTENTIONAL — the classical test, inapplicable. Current practice mostly runs STRICT by default while writing documents that read as if INTENTIONAL applied.

TENSION: READING A: agency law resolves the vacuum — the deployer is the bearer and the gap literature has been reinventing settled doctrine. READING B: it resolves the vacuum only by making the bearer's liability unbounded, which is not a resolution but a cost transfer, and it explains why deployers write terms shifting risk to users.

MISSING: Any verified formulation of a scope test applied to automated action. Any survey of whether deployment terms use enumerated permissions or open-ended authority.

BOUNDARY: This is a reading of doctrine, not legal advice or a claim about any jurisdiction's holdings. The transfer from human agency to software agency is precisely what courts have not settled.

CITATION TRAIL: [[FORAGE-PT-015]] and [[FORAGE-PT-039]] -> agency doctrine -> scope of authority as the failing test -> next: the moral crumple zone, which describes what happens when liability lands on an operator who lacked the discretion to prevent the outcome.

TEST: Collect ten deployment agreements for autonomous or semi-autonomous systems. Code each for how authority is delimited: enumerated permissions, outcome caps, or open. If most are open, the STRICT regime is operating unacknowledged and the documents are performing an intentional test that cannot run.
