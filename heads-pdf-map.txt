QUESTION: What kinds of conflict, rivalry, asymmetry, and temporary hierarchy increase a group's capacity to learn—and what kinds cause the self-organizing process to collapse?
THESIS: The social mechanism inside “self-organized learning” may have been misdescribed when it is narrated primarily as curiosity plus collaboration. Mitra's own observations contain rivalry, unequal status, “knows” and “know nots,” knowledge exchange, peer teaching, gender challenge, friendship, imitation, and strategically introduced perturbations. Abrams's later conflict framework supplies a different vocabulary for reading these dynamics: productive groups do not eliminate disagreement or asymmetry. They require conditions under which difference can remain active without becoming withdrawal, domination, or destructive conflict. The resulting hypothesis is stronger than “children teach each other”: SELF-ORGANIZATION MAY REQUIRE A CONFLICT METABOLISM. A learning group must be able to convert differences in knowledge, interpretation, status, confidence, and identity into further inquiry.
